Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

JPEC measure: Procedural Fairness. Passed every minimum standard JPEC applies to this kind of evaluation (5 of 5), including procedural fairness.
2. Follows the lawN/A
JPEC measure: Legal Ability. JPEC does not survey part-time judges; not counted.
3. Temperament & integrityN/A
JPEC measure: Integrity & Judicial Temperament. Not surveyed; not counted.
4. Runs the court wellN/A
JPEC measure: Administrative Skills. Not surveyed; not counted.
5. Public recordN/A
H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.
OverallC
Average of the graded questions (A=4 … F=0; N/A left out): 2.0. A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge and JPEC staff interview people leaving court. The judge must pass every minimum standard (5 of 5).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Appointed in 2014, Judge Carpenter received positive reviews from the observer and nearly all respondents. The judge presented a polite, sensitive demeanor and demonstrated respectful treatment of court participants. He also conveyed sound communication skills to ensure those in court received clear information and understood what to do. This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

How JPEC gathered this report

Judge Carpenter’s report consists of a two-and-a-half-hour observation conducted on two different days via WebEx videoconferencing. It also consists of 14 interviews conducted in-person and via telephone. The interview sample includes litigants and their friends and family members, court staff, and attorneys. Court staff may include clerks, bailiffs, interpreters, and service providers.

Overall

Judge Carpenter received positive reviews from the observer and nearly all respondents. The judge presented a polite, sensitive demeanor and demonstrated respectful treatment of court participants. He also conveyed sound communication skills to ensure those in court received clear information and understood what to do.

What most people said

The observer reported that Judge Carpenter exhibited a soft spoken, natural manner. He greeted court participants and said “please” and “thank you” to them on a routine basis. He dismissed them politely and worked well with attorneys on both sides. Judge Carpenter appeared to respect the value of individuals in court by validating their efforts. He often expressed appreciation when litigants fulfilled their obligations and demonstrated progress. He also demonstrated patience with all parties, particularly those that were experiencing problems or delays. On the other hand, the judge held litigants accountable for their actions. In one case, he admonished a litigant for being unprepared, but he did not get angry or embarrass anyone. Regarding another litigant who failed to provide information to the courts about his treatment, the judge emphasized that it was his responsibility to do so.

According to the observer, Judge Carpenter ensured that court participants received the necessary information and understood how to move forward. He spoke clearly, and he appeared to summarize matters well. He made the effort to explain a lot of information, such as how to prepare for an upcoming trial. During longer explanations, he did not always pause intermittently to ensure the litigant understood. But he was thorough in his explanations and did ensure all questions were answered. He was careful to see that litigants understood what they were electing to do, what rights they were giving up, and how they should proceed so they could avoid common mistakes. He seemed sincere about prioritizing sound communication so that he could save them trouble going forward.

Ten of the 14 respondents particularly emphasized Judge Carpenter’s “cordial” manner and respectful behavior. Three found the judge to be “polite,” with one sharing, “he doesn’t raise his voice or get upset with defendants.” Others variously described him as “kind,” “professional,” and “reasonable.” Six of the respondents found that he demonstrated respectful treatment of participants. One stated that he was “respectful” with their party, while another said he was “respectful of everybody.” Another appreciated his treatment of “defendants and [court] employees.”

Five respondents highlighted the judge’s concern for participants’ situations. They variously described him as “extremely concerned” and “understanding.” One shared, “he’s got feelings. He works with you. He feels you.” Another said, “he’s really concerned and considerate with people,” and “he looks at each defendant individually.”

Four spoke of the judge’s communication skills. Three referenced his ability to explain information, with one stating that he was “extremely detailed” in explaining people’s rights and “[ensuring] that defendants understand” them.

Three spoke of the judge’s neutrality. One described him as “thorough,” as one who “goes by the book,” and “treats everybody the same.” Another described him as “careful,” as one who “weights the evidence,” and “is very transparent.”

Comments that stood apart

One litigant responded that the judge “talked to me, but he didn’t let me talk” and added that the judge “seemed condescending.”

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Jon Carpenter was appointed to the Carbon County and Wellington City Justice Courts in May 2014 and to the East Carbon Justice Court in January 2017. Judge Carpenter received a bachelor's degree from the University of Utah and a law degree from Creighton University. After law school, Judge Carpenter served as a law clerk in the Utah Seventh District. Prior to taking the bench, Judge Carpenter practiced at a private law firm providing a wide range of legal services. Judge Carpenter is currently a member of the Board of Justice Court Judges.
