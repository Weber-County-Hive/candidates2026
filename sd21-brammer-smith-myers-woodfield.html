Senate District 21: The Primary a Judicial-Independence PAC Came For

Incumbent Brady Brammer defeated a primary challenger funded by a PAC formed specifically over concerns about judicial independence — the same concerns raised by bills Brammer himself sponsored. He now faces two little-funded opponents in November.

New here? Brady Brammer is the Republican incumbent for Senate District 21 (Highland, Utah County), first appointed to the seat in late 2024 and elected in his own right in 2024. This file covers his 2026 re-election: a Republican primary against Cedar Hills City Councilmember Kelly Smith, decided June 23, and a November 3 general election against Democratic nominee Kandee Myers and Forward Party candidate Wayne Woodfield. For Brammer's full legislative record — every bill he's sponsored, graded — see his profile in the Utah Senators series, linked below.

1. The Race

Brammer was selected by Utah County Republican Party delegates to fill Senate District 21 after Mike Kennedy's departure in late 2024, then won the seat outright in the 2024 general election. His 2026 re-election bid initially drew two Republican primary challengers — Kelly Smith and Seth Stewart — but Stewart withdrew in March 2026 to pursue a federal congressional race, leaving a two-person primary between Brammer and Smith, a Cedar Hills City Council member and retired educator.

Brammer won that June 23 primary. He advances to the November 3 general election against Kandee Myers, the Democratic nominee, and Wayne Woodfield of the Forward Party of Utah.

2. The Primary — Brammer vs. Kelly Smith

Republican · Cedar Hills City Council · Challenger

Kelly Smith

A Cedar Hills City Council member for five years and a retired educator, Smith ran on government transparency, fiscal conservatism, term limits, local control, and stronger communication with constituents. She was endorsed by more than two dozen mayors and city councilmembers from across the district. At a May 19 American Fork Chamber of Commerce debate, Smith argued the state had "moved too fast" on a major recent proposal and said "we need to slow things down" — a process-focused contrast with Brammer's record-of-results framing.

Smith raised roughly $65,000. Of that, only about 8% — roughly $5,147 — came from individual donors outside her own household. The rest was overwhelmingly self-loaned and PAC-funded: $20,725 in loans she made to her own campaign, $22,627.99 from the Checks and Balances PAC, and $16,500 in cash from a second PAC, Fresh Air PAC, headed by David Garbett.

3. The PAC Finding

A PAC formed specifically to defend judicial independence funded Smith's campaign against Brammer. The Checks and Balances PAC — which also files under the name "Rule of Law PAC" — gave Smith $14,627.99 in in-kind signature-gathering (April 21 and May 11, 2026) plus $8,000 in cash (May 21, 2026). The PAC's own stated purpose is defending an independent judiciary in Utah.

This is not a coincidence of timing, and it isn't even two separate stories — it's one continuous conflict. The diagram below traces how a single legislative fight led directly to the money working against Brammer's re-election.

The PAC's own Statement of Organization, filed with the state, shows it was created December 24, 2025 — shortly before the legislative session that produced SJR005, HB0366, and SB0134 — and names its officers as Chair Alexandra Dorius, a paralegal, and Treasurer Caroline Morris, a student, both based in Millcreek. This reads as a real citizen-organized PAC responding to a specific legislative fight, not an anonymous or corporate-backed operation. The same PAC also funded signature-gathering ($6,264.84) plus a further $1,000 cash payment for the primary opponent of Rep. Jordan Teuscher, HB0366's own House chief sponsor, and separately gave $4,000 to a third candidate, Chris Mcconey, not yet connected to any other thread in this research. Smith's primary campaign separately drew $16,500 from a second PAC, Fresh Air PAC, headed by David Garbett — not yet independently researched.

Put plainly: the fine landed on the PAC that was actively working against Brammer's re-election, in a case Brammer himself pushed for. He and Teuscher jointly asked the Lt. Governor's office to investigate the PAC over its late disclosure filings — the same PAC funding the primary challenges against them both — and the PAC was fined $1,000 in June 2026 as a result. Smith told KSL she was initially unaware "Rule of Law PAC" was an alternate name for "Checks and Balances," then confirmed the connection once informed.

For balance: Brammer's own campaign finance reports contain 17 deleted transaction entries across the 2024–2026 period — a disclosure-process issue worth weighing on both sides of this race, not just the PAC's late filing.

4. The General Election — Brammer vs. Myers vs. Woodfield

Click a name to expand their bio and funding story.

Democratic Nominee · Real Estate Broker

Kandee Myers

An independent-contractor real estate agent, currently Branch Broker for REMAX Lighthouse's new American Fork location (opened January 2026) under broker Cheri Salazar; previously an Associate Broker at REMAX Select since 2018, until that brokerage was sold to Salazar at the end of 2025. Runs her own entity, Kandee Myers Real Estate.

Myers raised $17,807.66, of which $15,000 — 84.2% — came from her own household: $5,000 from Myers herself and $10,000 loaned by her husband, Marvin Barentsen. The remaining roughly $2,800 came from about 20 small individual donors, many out of state, several in unusual fractional-cent amounts consistent with a round-up or micro-donation platform. Her spending has leaned toward Democratic-aligned civic organizations — Mormon Women for Ethical Government, the League of Women Voters, and the Utah County Democratic Party.

Forward Party · Software Architect

Wayne Woodfield

A Software Architect at RainFocus, a Lehi-based event-management software company, where he also trains other software engineers. Woodfield won the Forward Party of Utah's nomination unopposed, 6 votes to 0, at the Utah County convention in March 2026.

Woodfield raised $3,285.00 from 18 small individual donors, mostly neighbors in Lehi, with no PAC or institutional money. Two donors are notable only as intra-party connections rather than outside influence: Teri McCabe, the Forward Party's own elected member of the Provo City School Board, and David Hinckley, the party's own 2026 candidate for Utah County Council At-Large District B.

5. Brammer's Legislative Record

Brammer sponsored or floor-sponsored 18 bills graded in this project's full review of his record — the bills that graded well were narrow and touched a few thousand people at most; the bills that graded poorly reshaped power itself, from a fully governor-appointed backup court to the state's own environmental rulemaking authority. His campaign is also funded in part by the other architects of that same judicial-power program: Sen. Chris Wilson (SB0134's sponsor) and Sen. Mike McKell (sponsor of both halves of the panel-transfer scheme) each gave Brammer's campaign $5,000. Pre-primary reporting also shows a $5,000 donation from the Utah County Legislative PAC, led by Rep. Stephanie Gricius, along with $1,000 from Sen. Evan Vickers and $1,250 from former Utah House Speaker Greg Curtis.

Brammer is also registered as a director of Central Utah GOP House PAC via a separate nonprofit-corporation filing, alongside Rep. Cory Maloy and Jon Hawkins. Records show all three received $1,000 payments from that PAC within days of each other in December 2024. The PAC's actual officers under Utah election law are a different, though overlapping, group — Maloy, Rep. Bradley Daw, and Sen. Mike McKell as Treasurer — and its full multi-year history includes donor overlap with several other networks already documented in this project.

6. Scorecard — Rubric Grade & Campaign Promises vs. Record

Brammer is the only candidate in this race with a governing record to grade — Smith, Myers, and Woodfield have never held state office, so there is nothing yet to check their campaign statements against. His overall rubric grade below is a composite of the 18 bills graded in his full Senate profile, not a new assessment.

Power F Chief or Senate sponsor of the 2026 session's core judicial-power stack (SJR005, HB0366) — case-transfer power concentrated in the offices his own party controls, plus a fully governor-appointed backup court.
Transparency D Sponsored bills that shield commissioner votes (SB0233), make court-decision publication discretionary instead of mandatory (HB0366), and walk back a promised free-audio requirement (HB0540) — partly offset by HB0540's genuine public court-records database.
Financial Accountability C Real donor-industry overlaps across several bills (insurance, banking, litigation funding, EnergySolutions), balanced by at least one genuinely pro-consumer bill (SB0230) passed with bipartisan support.
Environmental Impact D Sponsored bills capping state environmental standards at the federal floor (SB0234) and wrapping refinery production data in a near-total secrecy shield (HB0575).
Community Impact D Sponsored the near-total removal of the public's right to sue over a nuisance (HB0591), a PID meeting-transparency loophole (HB0017), and an ADA-lawsuit bill that risks chilling legitimate accessibility enforcement (SB0068).

Campaign promises vs. record

Brammer's campaign site describes him as an attorney who represents cities and school districts, an adjunct professor of ethics and business law at BYU, a five-year member of the Highland City Planning Commission, and a board member of The Great Outdoors Fund. His 2026 campaign message centers on having "delivered results on tax cuts, conservative spending, public safety, local control and protecting Utah values." Four of those claims can be checked directly against bills he sponsored — click each to see what the record shows.

"Local control"

Two of his own sponsored bills move authority in the opposite direction: SJR005 and HB0366 concentrate case-transfer power in state-level offices (the AG, the Governor, the Legislature) rather than leaving it with locally assigned judges, and HB0017 broadens where a Public Infrastructure District can hold its meetings from the district's own boundaries out to its much-larger creating city or county — moving meetings further from, not closer to, the residents directly affected. Notably, Brammer himself served over five years on the Highland City Planning Commission, working directly on zoning and development issues — direct hands-on experience with exactly the kind of local land-use transparency question HB0017 weakens.

"Conservative spending"

Brammer's own bill, SB0235, began as a pay-raise bill for the Attorney General under a different sponsor; the substitute switched sponsorship to Brammer, retitled it for the Governor, and by enrollment produced a formula pegging the Governor's salary to the Chief Justice's — roughly a 47% raise using 2025 figures. He does separately cite a specific, checkable $1.5 billion tax-cut figure accumulated over six years, which this file has not independently verified bill-by-bill.

"Protecting access to the outdoors" (from his Great Outdoors Fund board role)

Sits in tension with two of his own sponsored bills: SB0234 caps Utah's environmental standards at the federal floor, and HB0575 wraps refinery production data in a near-total secrecy shield while creating a new tax-credit subsidy for pipeline and fossil-fuel storage infrastructure. Both bills graded D in the full rubric review.

"Cutting the gas tax by 15%" (from his campaign's "Record" page)

Accurate as far as it goes, but incomplete: this refers to HB0575, the same bill that simultaneously raised the tax rate on alternative fuels (CNG/LNG/hydrogen), wrapped refinery production data in a near-total secrecy shield, and created a new tax-credit subsidy for pipeline and fossil-fuel storage infrastructure. His campaign cites only the popular half of a bill whose other half earned a D grade in the full rubric review.

"Public safety" / "protecting Utah values"

Not clearly tied to any specific bill in his sponsored record documented in this file — too general to check against a specific action.

On Smith, Myers, and Woodfield: none of the three has held elected state office, so none has a legislative record to grade on this rubric or check against campaign statements. Smith campaigned on "government transparency, fiscal conservatism, term limits, local control, and stronger communication with constituents"; Myers's spending pattern suggests alignment with Democratic-aligned civic priorities but no detailed platform was captured in this research; Woodfield's platform beyond his party's general centrist positioning was not documented. All three remain untested promises, not a record — that distinction should stay clear in any published version of this comparison.

8. Questions Worth Asking

  • Should a PAC founded around a specific policy concern be required to disclose that founding purpose alongside its contributions, the way its name alone ("Checks and Balances") doesn't fully convey?
  • Did Brammer's request that the Lt. Governor's office investigate a PAC opposing his own primary — while his own campaign carried 17 deleted transaction entries — get equal scrutiny in return?
  • Brammer personally asked the state to investigate a PAC opposing him, and it was fined for a late disclosure. Separately, the PAC he co-directs, Central Utah GOP House PAC, paid $1,000 each to all three of its own directors — including Brammer himself — out of money it had collected. No wrongdoing has been found in that PAC's own filings, and self-directed PAC giving isn't unusual or necessarily improper. But it's a fair question to sit with: is the same appetite for scrutiny being applied to a PAC he controls that was applied to one working against him?
  • What would it take for a Forward Party or a genuinely independent candidate to be financially competitive in a race like this one?

9. Conclusion — What to Weigh Before Voting

This file isn't a recommendation. It's an attempt to lay out three separate things that don't always line up with each other, so a voter can weigh them for themselves rather than take any single one at face value:

  • What they say. Campaign sites, endorsements, and debate answers describe the candidate a campaign wants voters to see. Some of it checks out cleanly against the record — some of it, like a tax-cut claim built from one popular half of a two-sided bill, doesn't tell the whole story on its own.
  • What they do. Bills sponsored, votes cast, and legislative roles held are the part of the record that exists independent of any campaign's framing. This is where the reach imbalance in this project's rubric shows up most clearly: the bills that graded well were narrow, and the bills that graded poorly reshaped power itself.
  • Where the money flows. Who funds a campaign, who a candidate's own PACs pay, and who benefits on both ends of that flow is often the least visible part of a race to an ordinary voter — and, as this file shows, it can reveal both real conflicts (a PAC's founder fighting the exact bill a candidate sponsored) and real complexity (a citizen PAC with no corporate backing, or a self-funded campaign that looks concerning only until the self-funding is understood).

None of these three lenses alone tells the full story, and this project's standing rubric — weighing power, transparency, financial accountability, environmental impact, and community impact — exists precisely because a candidate's stated values, their actual votes, and their money trail don't always point the same direction. The collective good is served best when all three get checked before a ballot is cast, not just the one a campaign wants a voter to see.
