THE VOTE

Three Budgets, One Tie-Breaker

In Kaysville the five council members vote and the mayor presides. The mayor votes only to break a tie. On taxes, the record shows that happened once.

AUGUST 2023 · FY2024 BUDGET

Council raises property tax revenue 19.51%

Unanimous council vote. About $99 a year on a $640,000 home, per the Davis Journal.

JUNE 19, 2025 · FY2026 TENTATIVE BUDGET

2–2 tie. Tran casts the deciding vote.

The budget proposed "a 31.72% increase in Kaysville's portion of the property tax rate," about $14.61 a month on a $690,000 home. Adams and Hunt voted yea; Jackson and Blackham voted nay.

"With a two–two tie, Mayor Tran cast the deciding vote in favor of the motion. The motion passed with a final vote of three to two." — Kaysville City Council minutes

The minutes record that she called the increase "substantial" and argued for "more consistent use of the Truth in Taxation process to apply modest, inflation-aligned increases annually, potentially avoiding larger spikes in future years."

2025

State Tax Commission denies the increase

Kaysville was one of 35 entities denied. KSL reported the city sought about $1.8 million, and that denials that year turned on compliance with new procedural rules.

AUGUST 2026 · FY2027 BUDGET

A smaller increase, cut before adoption

Tentative budget: $1,226,151 in new property tax. Final: $802,910, about $6.39 a month on the average home. The budget message states: "Any increase in the General Fund budget due to the rise in inflation is proposed to be covered by property tax."

Kaysville property tax revenue, by budget year

General Fund current-year property tax, from the city's FY2027 budget document. FY24–25 are actuals; FY26 is what the city budgeted before the state denied the increase; FY27 is the adopted budget including the $802,910 Truth in Taxation increase.

"I'm very proud of our council and our staff and our departments because we all worked together to get it as close as we could... We don't have a larger commercial base." — Tran to the Standard-Examiner, Aug. 2026

The FY2027 cut of roughly a third between tentative and final budgets is part of the record, as is the city's small commercial tax base she points to.

HER OTHER SEATS

Mayor, Board Chair, Presiding Officer

Her form lists 19 board and leadership seats. Several put her on both sides of the same table.

Davis Technical College Worth knowing

Tran chairs Davis Tech's Board of Trustees. On Sept. 3, 2026, Davis Tech's vice president presented the college's ~30-year campus master plan to the Kaysville council she presides over, including a proposed Laurelwood Drive connection neighbors have raised traffic concerns about. The plan was set to go to the Davis Tech Board for approval on Sept. 24. The council's conflict-of-interest call followed the presentation; "no conflicts were identified." The minutes record no mention of her Davis Tech role. It was a presentation, not a vote, and her board seat is on her public form.

Lakeview Hospital

Her form lists the Lake View Hospital Board of Trustees. Lakeview is a MountainStar/HCA hospital. Its CEO, Troy Wood, endorsed her; MountainStar Healthcare Good Government PAC gave her $1,000.

The $6.5 million bond

On Sept. 17, 2026, the council authorized up to $6.5M in sales tax revenue bonds for the Operations Center (estimated total repayment $9,166,725). The resolution makes the mayor one of three officials who can choose the underwriter and set final terms, and she signs the documents. Zions Public Finance is the city's advisor and Zions Bancorporation the trustee. The city manager said repayment comes from previously approved utility rate increases; the FY27 budget sets aside about $474,000 a year across utility funds and the Road Fund. Public hearing: Nov. 5, 2026. The process meets the Local Government Bonding Act's notice requirements.

Baywin's filed address is the same Boise address as an early $20 donor, Weston Yates.

$455.64 of his early money came from outside Utah (Idaho, California, Virginia). Several of his entries are marked as amendments.

His platform

Per his campaign site: he moved to Utah in 2019 and has lived in Syracuse for three years. He spent a decade in retail, describes himself as "the son of two union members," and now works as "Head of Portfolio Management at a financial tech company." His disclosure form names the employer as Founderpath Inc. of Austin, Texas, where he underwrites business loans "primarily serving software as a service start up companies" and manages the loan book. The form lists no businesses he owns, no stocks or bonds over $5,000, and no board seats. He lists three priorities:

Affordability: "Lower the real costs that hit your paycheck so families can stop getting by and start getting ahead." · Education: "Stop asking our schools to do more with less." · The Great Salt Lake: "Save the lake. A healthy lake means clean air, strong property values, and a stable Utah economy."

His priorities page gets specific on the lake: he backs the Utah Rivers Council's "4,200 Project" to return the lake to 4,200 feet, says he "will fight against any legislation that silences the public or removes health checks from water rights decisions," and names Grow the Flow as a group whose lead he would follow on getting "wet water" to the lake. On affordability he lists housing supply, prescription price gouging and childcare costs.

Like Tran, he runs on affordability. Unlike Tran, he has no government voting record to compare it with. He addresses water and the Great Salt Lake directly; Tran's platform doesn't mention either.

The gap

Tran's campaign reported about 48 times Neal's cash ($81,735 vs. $1,688). Counting Meta's $152,503 in outside spending on her behalf, money spent to elect her runs about 139 times his cash.
