Six Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSA long-serving senator with a large, mostly institutional campaign account faces a first-time Democratic candidate running on small gifts. Both have ties to the same school district, and Grover’s bills have reshaped how the districts formed from Alpine’s split borrow and are run.

  1. Grover has raised $225,330.45 in new money since 2022. About 40.6% came from party and candidate committees, led by $72,500 from the Utah Republican Senate Campaign Committee; about 49.7% came from businesses, PACs and associations; about 9.8% came from individuals. He also carried over $66,722.03 from his 2018 campaign.
  2. His biggest vendor’s co-owner helped present his bill. The campaign paid Majority 51 $58,286.27 from 2022 to 2025 and paid a payee filed as “Richard Jaussi” $2,250. Richard Jaussi, Salt Lake County’s chief deputy auditor, lists himself as a co-owner of Majority 51 on his 2026 county disclosure, the state registry lists him as its governing person, and he helped present Grover’s S.B. 236 in both the Senate and House committees.
  3. Most of his spending is filed under one broad label. 87% of the $155,894.84 he spent is listed as “Campaign Expense.” Most of it ($139,435.69) went out in 2022, when he won a primary with 69.5%. Since then he has spent $16,459.15.
  4. Grover’s school bills and Alpine’s split. His S.B. 188 (2025) exempted reorganized districts’ lease revenue bonds from a borrowing cap, and Alpine then brought $201 million in bonds to market. His S.B. 326 (2026), presented with the Lake Mountain School Board president, lets new districts’ boards set their first leaders’ terms at up to about four years.
  5. Both candidates have Alpine School District on their forms. Grover lists it as a previous employer (“Administrator”). Smith lists it as his current employer (music teacher at Timpanogos High School), and his spouse’s.
  6. Smith has raised $4,454.44 and spent $2,821.03. 24 of his 38 gifts were $50 or less; his largest were five $500 gifts, one from the Operating Engineers union. One payee is filed only as “Peter,” and his disclosure form lists a piano-service business and a condo.

S.B. 188 (2025): the borrowing cap exemption

S.B. 188 (2025): the borrowing cap exemption

S.B. 86 (2024) had capped how much school districts could borrow through lease revenue bonds, which don’t go to voters. Grover’s S.B. 188, “School District Modifications,” was signed March 17, 2025. The exemption was added in the House Education Committee on Feb. 27, 2025, in an amendment moved by Rep. Matt MacPherson (12–0). It says a lease revenue bond issued by a new or reorganized district after voters in the divided district rejected a general obligation bond, where voters in the new area had approved it, “is not subject to the combined total limitation” “due to the prior approval of voters within the new or reorganized new school district.”

  • Rep. Stephanie Gricius presented it in the House committee “with the assistance of Julie King, Alpine School District Board of Education,” per the minutes.
  • Every recorded vote on the bill was unanimous. The steps that changed its content, including a Senate floor substitute and the adoption of a conference committee’s version on the session’s last day, were voice votes.
  • That July, Alpine School District brought $201 million in lease revenue bonds to market, with the debt to be assumed by a new district after the 2027 split, as published by the district.
  • In 2026, Rep. Tiara Auxier’s H.B. 332 would have required a voter-approved bond before any school district lease revenue bond. Rep. Jordan Teuscher moved to hold it in committee after three school-district officials spoke against it, and it died at 11:59 p.m. on March 6.

Graded on the Bill-Tracker’s water, land and local power page (S.B. 188, F; H.B. 332, A).

Read from the status history, House and Senate Education minutes and the amendment text; the conference committee’s compare document was not read
S.B. 326 (2026): longer first terms for new school board leaders

S.B. 326 (2026): longer first terms for new school board leaders

School board presidents and vice presidents normally serve two-year terms. Grover’s S.B. 326, signed March 19, 2026, adds that the board “of a new school district” may set its first president’s and vice president’s terms as the rest of the year they’re sworn in “plus one additional calendar year” or “plus three additional calendar years.” The existing rule that a board can remove an officer for cause by a two-thirds vote is unchanged.

  • March 2, 2026: Grover presented it to the Senate Education Committee “with the assistance of Julie King, Lake Mountain School Board President,” per the draft minutes. No one else is recorded speaking. It passed 4–0 (Fillmore, McKell and Wilson absent). The committee’s Feb. 27 minutes note the bill “was not discussed” at that meeting.
  • March 3: the Senate passed it 27–0 on second and third reading together under a suspension of the rules.
  • March 6: the House passed it 56–5 with Rep. Gricius as floor sponsor (no votes: Reps. Dominguez, Hollins, Moss, Nguyen and D. Owens).

Worth seeing side by side: Lake Mountain is one of the new districts formed from Alpine’s split. S.B. 236 (2026): property tax exemption deadlines, and who helped present it

S.B. 236 (2026): property tax exemption deadlines, and who helped present it

Grover’s S.B. 236, “Property Tax Exemption Process Amendments” (House floor sponsor Rep. Doug Shallenberger, whose district is in SD-23), rewrites how county boards of equalization handle exemption applications, taking effect Jan. 1, 2027. Per the first substitute, the version that passed, it:

  • requires a written decision by the date the county assessor must deliver the assessment book, instead of May 1, and for newly acquired property, by the later of May 15 or 90 days after filing (up from 45);
  • requires each written decision, including a revocation, to tell the owner of the right to appeal to the State Tax Commission;
  • lets the board ask for more information instead of, or as well as, a hearing, and gives it express power to revoke an exemption after notice if the property may no longer qualify; and
  • when an owner misses the yearly statement, requires the board to revoke the exemption and notify the owner by April 1, instead of sending notice of intent to revoke, with a late-filing window through March 31 for a medical emergency, a death or other extraordinary circumstances.

What changed from the introduced bill: as introduced, an applicant could also appeal to the Tax Commission if the county board failed to issue a written decision on time. The substitute removed that; an appeal now follows a written decision the owner disagrees with.

Votes: the Senate Revenue and Taxation Committee adopted the substitute and recommended it, 5–0 each (Feb. 5, Sen. Chris Wilson moving). The Senate passed it 24–0 on Feb. 23; the House Revenue and Taxation Committee recommended it 9–0 on March 2 (Reps. Koford, Kyle and Okerlund voting yes); the House passed it 66–1 on March 6 (Rep. Leah Hansen voting no).

H.B. 195 (2026): a gold- and silver-backed payment system

H.B. 195 (2026): a gold- and silver-backed payment system

Grover was Senate floor sponsor of Rep. Ken Ivory’s H.B. 195, “Precious Metals Amendments,” which became law March 26, 2026 without the governor’s signature. As enrolled, it:

  • requires the state treasurer, with the Division of Finance, to contract with one or more third parties, through a request for proposals, to set up and run “a precious metals-backed electronic payment system” backed by physical gold or silver held in vaults in Utah, which the state can use to pay contractors who choose it;

Its 23 House cosponsors include Reps. Jason Kyle (HD-8) and Jake Sawyer (HD-9).

THE RUBRIC

Graded Topic by Topic

IN PLAIN WORDSEach topic gets a grade, A (best) to F (worst), on five questions: Does it check power? Can the public see it? Is it clear who pays? What does it do to air, water and land? Do the people affected get a real say? N/A means the question doesn’t apply.

How to read these: grades judge only what has already happened in the filings as of Sept. 24, 2026. They judge the record, not the person. Scale: C means the record meets what the law requires. B or A means it goes beyond that in a way the record shows. D means required information is there but the key part is vague or bundled. F means required information is missing.

How the grades work

Every topic is graded on the same five questions, written once for this site and used on every page:

  • Power: Does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?
  • Transparency: Can the public actually see the process: posted hearings, itemized financials, disclosed conflicts, regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?
  • Financial accountability: Who actually pays, who’s protected from paying, and is that structure disclosed plainly or buried in a bundled figure?
  • Environmental impact: What’s the effect on water, wetlands and wildlife, and does the process treat that as a real constraint or a box to check?
  • Community impact: Who bears the cost or holds a historical or cultural stake in this decision, tribal or otherwise, and did the process give them real standing to be heard, or was their interest treated as a formality?

The scale: A goes well beyond what the law requires, in ways the record shows. B goes beyond the requirement. C meets what the law requires, and no more. D has the required information, but the key part is vague or lumped into a catch-all. F is missing required information, or removes a public check with nothing in its place. N/A means the question doesn’t fit the topic.

Ground rules: each topic is graded on its own, not averaged into one score for the person. Grades judge the record, not the person’s reputation or other conduct. Where how something was built and how it turned out differ, both are noted. Meeting the legal minimum earns a C, not a B.
