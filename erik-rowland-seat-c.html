"I have no conflict of interest to report" — Jan. 2, 2026

Erik Rowland has served on the Wasatch County Council since January 2023 and is running unopposed for reelection to Seat C in 2026. In May 2026, the governor appointed him to the Military Installation Development Authority (MIDA) board — a state authority with its own project areas, its own public infrastructure districts, and its own statutory disclosure requirement for the people who sit on it.

Utah Code requires MIDA board members to file a conflict-of-interest disclosure statement every January, publicly posted on MIDA's own website. Failing to do so can be a class B misdemeanor with a referral to the Attorney General. MIDA's board page links to every voting member's disclosure — and all but two of them, Rowland's and Froerer's, are genuine MIDA-branded statutory forms.

His county campaign has raised nothing and spent only the $265.66 filing fee — a no-fundraising, unopposed race with no opponent to raise any of this. That's part of why it sits on the public record rather than in the campaign conversation this fall.

What the other board members filed

MIDA's actual disclosure form is detailed: current and previous employers, any entity where the filer is or was an officer or owner, income sources over $5,000, stock and bond holdings over $5,000, and — critically — "each entity or organization... in which the individual currently serves, or served in the preceding year, in a paid leadership capacity or in a paid or unpaid position on a board of directors." It also asks for a spouse's name and employment history.

Board member Gary Harter's 2026 filing shows the form working as intended: he disclosed his seats on MIDA's five associated public infrastructure districts, the Utah Defense Alliance, NASDVA, the State Workforce Development Board, and the Timpanogos Valley Theater. That's what the form is built to capture.

Rowland is a Wasatch County Councilman; Froerer is a Weber County Commissioner. Both sit on MIDA, and both filed their own home county's generic conflict-of-interest form in place of MIDA's actual disclosure — different documents, same underlying substitution. MIDA re-hosted Froerer's under its own web address, while Rowland's links out to Wasatch County's site directly. Neither is the statutory MIDA form every other board member filed.

That county form — dated January 2, 2026 — asks a simpler question: whether the filer believes any relationship "could contribute to a conflict of interest," with room to specify boards, businesses, and an employer if so. Rowland checked "I have no conflict of interest to report" and left every line blank, including no mention of a spouse, despite the form's own instructions asking for boards "you (and your spouse) sit on."

MIDA's website uses that same document to cover his disclosure across all four of his MIDA-linked positions — the board seat itself, plus three public infrastructure districts (Mountain Village PID, Golf and Equestrian Center PID, and Cormont PID). One blank form, filed once, standing in for four separate disclosure obligations.

A second board, a starker gap

Rowland also sits on the Wasatch Open Lands Board (WOLB), which reviews conservation easement applications and recommends how the county's Open Space bond money gets spent. WOLB has seven members: Rowland, fellow councilman Karl McMillan, Chair Heidi Franco (Heber City's mayor), Dallin Koecher, a Midway representative, and two citizen-at-large appointees.

WOLB's own page, on the county's own website, lists disclosure forms for exactly three of those seven members — Koecher, Franco, and Tracy Taylor. Rowland's name has no form linked at all. Neither do McMillan's or the board's Midway representative's. This isn't a case of the wrong form standing in, the way it is on MIDA's site — on WOLB's own official page, there's nothing posted for him at all, and no version was ever filed.

WOLB's form, drawn from a separate state statute than MIDA's, is sharper than the generic county form Rowland did file for his MIDA seat. Beyond the standard questions about business interests and entities that do business with the county, it includes a rule written specifically for this board: a property is ineligible for open-space bond funding if a board member — or their spouse, parent, sibling, or child — holds a 10% or greater interest in it, or if a member was offered any gift or payment to represent the property's owner during the selection process. Rowland owns a development LLC. No filed disclosure means no record of whether that rule was ever considered.

A different kind of enforcement gap

Fellow MIDA board member Gage Froerer's separate, county-level Nordic Valley disclosure has been formally reviewed by county attorneys and the state auditor, who found it compliant. His MIDA disclosure — the same wrong county form documented above — has not been reviewed at all, and neither has Rowland's. Rowland's other gaps (the CAMS board seat, the business entities) have also never been formally reviewed by anyone. That's a weaker starting point than Froerer's Nordic Valley case: not something that was checked and cleared, but something that has never been checked at all.

Where this stands

Rowland was not asked for comment, consistent with this publication's practice of reporting from public records and documents in hand. He is running unopposed for Seat C this November.
