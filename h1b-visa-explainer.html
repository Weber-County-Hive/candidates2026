How the H-1B Visa Actually Works

A skilled-worker visa that runs on a lottery, a $100,000 fee now blocked in court, and a rulebook Congress keeps trying to rewrite.

← Back to Kennedy v. Larsen case file

What It Is

The H-1B is an employer-sponsored, temporary visa for "specialty occupations" — jobs that normally require at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. It's the main legal pathway U.S. companies use to hire skilled foreign professionals for roles they say they can't easily fill domestically. Visas run up to 3 years initially, renewable once for a total of 6 years (longer in some green-card-pending cases).

Who Holds Them

The program skews heavily toward tech: software engineers, data scientists, and IT roles make up the large majority of H-1B holders, followed by healthcare (physicians, especially in underserved areas) and academic research. By nationality, Indian nationals hold roughly 70% of H-1B visas, with Chinese nationals a distant second. Big tech companies and IT-consulting/outsourcing firms are consistently the largest sponsors by volume.

How Someone Gets One

  1. Employer registers the candidate

    In early March, the employer creates a USCIS account and submits an electronic registration for each prospective hire, paying a registration fee (currently $215/person). Each candidate can only be entered once per year, even if multiple employers want to sponsor them.

  2. USCIS runs the lottery

    Only 85,000 new H-1B visas are issued each year — 65,000 in the "regular" cap plus 20,000 reserved for people with a U.S. master's degree or higher. Demand is far higher than that, so USCIS selects registrations to advance. Starting with the FY2027 cycle (spring 2026), selection is no longer purely random — it's now weighted so higher-wage job offers have better odds.

  3. Selected candidates file the full petition

    Winners get a filing window (at least 90 days) to submit the actual H-1B petition, along with a certified Labor Condition Application from the Department of Labor confirming the wage and working conditions meet legal requirements.

  4. USCIS approves or denies

    USCIS reviews the petition for fraud, wage compliance, and whether the job genuinely qualifies as a specialty occupation. If approved, the visa is authorized to start no earlier than October 1 of that fiscal year.

  5. The $100,000 fee: on the books, blocked in court

    A September 2025 presidential proclamation added a $100,000 fee per petition for workers coming from abroad, on top of existing filing costs — the provision several members of Congress, including Kennedy, have introduced bills to carve exceptions into, particularly for healthcare workers. Federal courts have since blocked it. In June 2026 a federal court in Massachusetts ruled the policy unlawful in a case brought by 20 states. On Sept. 30, 2026, U.S. District Judge Haywood S. Gilliam Jr. in California struck down the agency policies that set the fee and barred enforcement, including under a Sept. 18, 2026 proclamation that extended the fee through Sept. 21, 2027. As of October 2026, employers are not being required to pay it while the cases continue.

The Acronyms — Tap to Flip

H-1B

Specialty Occupation Visa
A temporary work visa category under INA Section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(b) for jobs requiring at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field.
USCIS
Tap to flip
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services
The federal agency (under DHS) that runs the H-1B lottery and approves or denies petitions.
LCA
Tap to flip
Labor Condition Application
A filing certified by the Department of Labor confirming the employer will pay the required wage and meet working-condition rules before an H-1B petition can be filed.
I-129
Tap to flip
Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker
The actual USCIS form an employer files after winning the lottery to formally request H-1B status for the worker.
DOL
Tap to flip
Department of Labor
Certifies the Labor Condition Application, verifying the offered wage meets the required minimum before an H-1B petition can move forward.
INA
Tap to flip
Immigration and Nationality Act
The underlying federal law (as amended) that creates the H-1B category and every other U.S. visa classification.
OPT
Tap to flip
Optional Practical Training
Temporary work authorization for F-1 international students after graduation, often used as a bridge until an H-1B petition can be filed.
CPT
Tap to flip
Curricular Practical Training
Work authorization for F-1 students that takes place while still enrolled in school, distinct from OPT which happens after graduation.
STEM OPT
Tap to flip
STEM Optional Practical Training Extension
A 24-month extension of OPT for graduates in Science, Technology, Engineering, or Mathematics fields, giving more time to be selected in the H-1B lottery.
