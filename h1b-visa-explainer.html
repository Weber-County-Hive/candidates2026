How the H-1B Visa Actually Works

A skilled-worker visa that runs on a lottery, a $100,000 new fee, and a rulebook Congress keeps trying to rewrite.

← Back to Kennedy v. Larsen case file

What It Is

The H-1B is an employer-sponsored, temporary visa for "specialty occupations" — jobs that normally require at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. It's the main legal pathway U.S. companies use to hire skilled foreign professionals for roles they say they can't easily fill domestically. Visas run up to 3 years initially, renewable once for a total of 6 years (longer in some green-card-pending cases).

Who Holds Them

The program skews heavily toward tech: software engineers, data scientists, and IT roles make up the large majority of H-1B holders, followed by healthcare (physicians, especially in underserved areas) and academic research. By nationality, Indian nationals hold roughly 70% of H-1B visas, with Chinese nationals a distant second. Big tech companies and IT-consulting/outsourcing firms are consistently the largest sponsors by volume.

How Someone Gets One

  1. Employer registers the candidate

    In early March, the employer creates a USCIS account and submits an electronic registration for each prospective hire, paying a registration fee (currently $215/person). Each candidate can only be entered once per year, even if multiple employers want to sponsor them.

  2. USCIS runs the lottery

    Only 85,000 new H-1B visas are issued each year — 65,000 in the "regular" cap plus 20,000 reserved for people with a U.S. master's degree or higher. Demand is far higher than that, so USCIS selects registrations to advance. Starting with the FY2027 cycle (spring 2026), selection is no longer purely random — it's now weighted so higher-wage job offers have better odds.

  3. Selected candidates file the full petition

    Winners get a filing window (at least 90 days) to submit the actual H-1B petition, along with a certified Labor Condition Application from the Department of Labor confirming the wage and working conditions meet legal requirements.

  4. USCIS approves or denies

    USCIS reviews the petition for fraud, wage compliance, and whether the job genuinely qualifies as a specialty occupation. If approved, the visa is authorized to start no earlier than October 1 of that fiscal year.

  5. Pay the new $100,000 fee, if it applies

    As of a September 2025 presidential proclamation, employers filing H-1B petitions for workers coming from abroad must pay a $100,000 fee per petition, on top of existing filing costs — the provision several members of Congress, including Kennedy, have introduced bills to carve exceptions into, particularly for healthcare workers.
