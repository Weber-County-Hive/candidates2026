How Judges Get on Your Ballot

Utah voters never pick a judge. They decide whether to keep one. Here is how a judge gets the seat, who grades them before you vote, what the grades mean, and the rules that changed in 2026.

Posted Oct. 17, 2026 · Updated Oct. 17, 2026.

The four steps

Utah’s courts describe judge selection in four steps: nomination, appointment, confirmation and retention election. A nominating commission screens applicants and sends a short list; the governor appoints from that list; the Utah Senate confirms; then, at the end of each term, voters decide whether to keep the judge.

Justice courts are different. Justice courts are city and county courts for traffic cases, misdemeanors and small claims. Their judges are named locally: for example, Judge Trent Nelson was named in 2016 by the Weber County Nominating Commission, then certified by the Utah Judicial Council. Only voters served by that court see that judge on the ballot.

What your vote does

Judges are not running against anyone and generally do not campaign; the law does let a judge form one campaign committee (see money rules). More Yes than No votes keeps the judge; more No than Yes ends the judge’s service at the end of the term. Judges usually face voters every six years; Supreme Court justices every ten.

Utah voters almost always say yes. No Utah Supreme Court justice has lost a retention election since at least 1990, and justices have been retained with an average of 80.6% of the vote (Ballotpedia). In Weber County in 2020, every judge on the ballot was kept, with Yes votes from 73.5% to 83.7%.

Who grades judges before you vote

The Judicial Performance Evaluation Commission (JPEC) has 13 members appointed by all three branches of state government, and has evaluated judges on the ballot since 2012. For most judges it surveys attorneys, court staff and other professionals who worked with the judge, and sends trained observers into the courtroom. Part-time justice court judges are not surveyed because too few attorneys appear before them; JPEC uses observers and, for some, short interviews with people leaving court.

Survey scores run from 1 to 5. The legal minimum is 3.6 in each scored area. JPEC also compares every judge with peers on the same kind of court and labels each area as well above, above, consistent with, below or well below peers. The commission then votes on whether the judge meets its standards.

One thing JPEC can’t show you: judges who score poorly often retire instead of facing voters, and their evaluations are never published. JPEC’s own data from the 2022 cycle found judges who scored well below their peers were about 25% more likely to step down.

How the Hive grades judges

The Hive’s collective rubric was built for bills, boards and campaign money, so judges get their own rubric. It uses the same letter scale and the same “C means meets the law” baseline, and it is applied the same way to every judge.

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

Money rules for judges

Under Utah Code Title 20A, Chapter 12, Part 3, a judge may set up one personal campaign committee for a retention election. Each contribution must be reported to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days of receipt. An interim report is due seven days before the general election (Oct. 27, 2026), and a summary report is due Jan. 10 of the next year. A judge who doesn’t file the interim report can be removed from the ballot. As of Oct. 17, 2026, the Hive had not found a retention committee for any judge on this ballot; this will be checked again after Oct. 27.

Discipline

The Judicial Conduct Commission investigates complaints against Utah judges. Its proceedings are confidential; only recommendations of discipline sent to the Utah Supreme Court, and orders the Court makes public, are released. As of Oct. 17, 2026, none of the 57 judges on this ballot appears on the commission’s public dispositions list, which runs from 1997 to 2026.

What changed in 2026

  • S.B. 134 expanded the Utah Supreme Court from five to seven justices, added Court of Appeals and district judgeships, and made the chief justice a governor’s appointment with Senate consent instead of a choice by the justices.
  • H.B. 392 lets the Attorney General, Governor or Legislature move a lawsuit challenging a state law to a panel of three district judges from different districts. See the HD-26 case file.
  • S.B. 233 changed judicial performance evaluations, including new timeliness standards for appellate judges, and keeps individual commissioners’ votes on a judge confidential.
  • H.B. 540 requires judges and court commissioners to file annual financial disclosures that will be posted online. The Judicial Council is setting the rules.
  • H.B. 262 (not passed) would have required 67% Yes for any judge to stay, instead of a simple majority. See the HD-8 case file.
