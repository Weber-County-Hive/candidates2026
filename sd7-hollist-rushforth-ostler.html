Nine Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSMost of the money in Hollist's campaign came from two places: one Fruit Heights donor and Hollist herself. Utah has no cap on how much one person can give a legislative candidate, so this is allowed. Rushforth's campaign is small and runs on gifts from $1 to $1,000. Ostler's is his own money.

  1. One donor, eight gifts, $200,248. Kayden Bell of Fruit Heights gave Hollist's campaign $25,000 on Dec. 30, 2025, then $175,248 more in 2026, the largest $50,000 on June 25, two days after the primary. That is 57% of everything the campaign took in and 85% of the money from donors other than Hollist.
  2. Hollist loaned her campaign $113,677.32. That includes $100,000 on Dec. 30, 2025, the same day as Bell's first gift. Her reports show $11,752.32 paid back to her, leaving $101,925 not shown as repaid.
  3. The campaign started 2026 with $127,100 in the bank. That balance came from the Year-End 2025 report: her $100,000 loan, Bell's $25,000 and $2,100 from two other donors.
  4. 102 other donors gave $35,049. Sixty-four of the 103 donors gave $100 or less in all. Three gave from outside Utah.
  5. No PAC, business or caucus money. Every dollar not from Hollist came from individual people, except bank interest and a $300 convention deposit the state party returned.
  6. $99,930.12 to Arena on July 15. The single largest payment is listed as "Advertising," three weeks after the primary. Other large payees: Apollo ($49,023), Empower Public Affair for signature gathering ($28,800) and Sun Print Solutions ($28,047.12).
  7. Rushforth raised $6,606.37. 59 individuals and the Planned Parenthood PAC ($100). He has spent $2,150.77, mostly on signs and supplies.
  8. Ostler's campaign is his own money. $235.46 in from himself and $1,395.46 out, every payment to himself. A $1,160 payment on Sept. 29 has no money in to match it.
  9. All three disclosure forms were filed. Hollist lists her employer, its stock and her husband's employer. Rushforth lists his teaching job and "N/a" to the rest. Ostler lists himself as retired, with income from "Social Security - Self - Business" and a board seat at Mineral Resources International of Ogden.

How Hollist got here. Adams, Senate president since 2019, ran for re-election. Hollist, Braden Hess and Adams were on the June 23 Republican primary ballot. Election-night results: Hollist about 43%, Adams 35%, Hess 22% (Salt Lake Tribune, June 23, 2026). The Tribune reported Hollist had spent more than $181,000 and Adams $429,000. Coverage tied the result in part to Adams's vote, as a member of the Military Installation Development Authority (MIDA) board, on the Stratos data center in Box Elder County (KUTV; Utah News Dispatch).

Who Hollist is. An attorney who moved to Fruit Heights from Harrisville at age 10 and has lived in Fruit Heights and Kaysville for 38 years; Weber State political science graduate; University of Utah law degree. Weber State's general counsel for many years; now vice president and general counsel of Arnold Machinery Company (Standard-Examiner, June 13, 2026; her disclosure form). First run for office.

Who Rushforth is. A junior high social studies and theater teacher in the Davis School District (his disclosure form). The Ballot Brief found no campaign website as of Sept. 8, 2026.

Who Ostler is. Jeffrey Ostler of Layton is the Constitution Party nominee, retired and a past candidate for state auditor, treasurer and the Utah House (see his section). The Ballot Brief found no campaign website as of Sept. 8, 2026.

IN PLAIN WORDSHollist put her own money in as loans, which the campaign can repay her later. Most of her small loans have been repaid. The $100,000 loan had not been repaid as of Sept. 25.

Own money isn't graded on this site; it's stated so nothing goes unsaid. All seven of her small loans match a payment of the same amount on the same day, and one is labeled "Candidate paid expense." That is how a candidate reports paying a campaign bill personally.

THE GRADES

Graded Topic by Topic

IN PLAIN WORDSEach topic gets a grade, A (best) to F (worst), on five questions: Does it check power? Can the public see it? Is it clear who pays? What does it do to air, water and land? Do the people affected get a real say? N/A means the question doesn't fit. A dash means the record isn't documented enough to grade.

How to read these: grades judge only what is already in the record as of Oct. 2, 2026. They judge the record, not the person. A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the candidate went beyond it, and the record shows it. Power on campaign money uses a fixed scale, the same for every candidate and party: A when 90% or more of the counted money comes from individual people, B at 67–89%, C at 34–66%, D at 10–33%, F under 10%. Party help and the candidate's own money are shown but not counted; own money is always stated. D means something is vague or bundled, or a public check is weakened; F means something required is missing, or a check is removed with nothing in its place. Candidates can amend a report at any time; when the record changes, the grade is updated and dated. Corrections are welcome at webercountyhive@gmail.com.

Overall is the average of the graded questions (A = 4, B = 3, C = 2, D = 1, F = 0; N/A and dashes are left out). A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters, and an average exactly halfway counts toward the higher letter with a minus.
