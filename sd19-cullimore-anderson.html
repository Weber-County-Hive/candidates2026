Seven Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSThe Senate’s No. 2 leader, backed mostly by business and professional groups, faces a Democratic candidate backed mostly by individuals. Both are checked the same way: money in, money out, disclosure form, stated positions. Only he has a voting record.

  1. Cullimore raised $114,250.00 in 2026. 93.9% came from businesses, PACs and associations, about 35.0% from outside Utah, and $2,000 in two gifts from individuals. His largest donor is a law firm, Robert J. DeBry & Associates ($15,000); the Utah Association for Justice, the trial lawyers’ group, and Nexiuum, LLC gave $10,000 each.
  2. He spent $38,510.00 through June 7, $21,500.00 of it to other Republican campaigns, and $7,468.29 on travel.
  3. His form lists a law practice, the presidency of Sentinel Security Life and four boards. He sits on the Senate Business and Labor Committee, which handles insurance bills. The state registry lists two more companies, COLLEX, LLC (with him as a member) and Fuerza Legal LLC (with his company KCJR as a member); his form lists neither.
  4. His site says “We must continue to encourage open meetings rules.” H.B. 530 (2025), which he carried in the Senate, exempts the Nucleus Institute from the open-meetings and records laws. The final version of H.B. 276 (2026), a substitute the bill page lists him as sponsoring, does not include the original bill’s minimum damages for people depicted in AI-made intimate images.
  5. Anderson raised $23,988.70 in 2026. 94.2% came from 158 individual gifts, 125 of them $100 or less. Two unions and a party club gave $1,400.
  6. She spent $9,831.73 through June 9, mostly on printing ($3,046) and two campaign firms ($5,000).
  7. Her form lists no outside employer (“Stay at home mom”), business or board, and one stock: Mineral Rite, a mining and reclamation company. She also listed her spouse’s employers, a defense contractor and the Utah National Guard.

H.B. 276 (2026): AI-made intimate images, and the final version

H.B. 276 (2026): AI-made intimate images, and the final version

Rep. Ariel Defay’s H.B. 276, “Artificial Intelligence Modifications,” was signed March 24, 2026 and takes effect Jan. 1, 2027. Cullimore was its Senate sponsor, and the bill page lists him as sponsor of the second substitute, the version that became law. As enrolled, it:

  • creates the Digital Voyeurism Prevention Act: an AI “generation service” may not distribute a “counterfeit intimate image” of a real person without that person’s consent, kept on record for seven years;
  • lets the person depicted sue for actual damages, including emotional distress, punitive damages for willful, reckless or malicious violations, and attorney fees, within three years of discovery or 10 years of the violation;
  • requires “covered platforms,” as the federal Take It Down Act defines them, to remove reported images within 48 hours;
  • gives generation services a safe harbor if they keep and publish a written policy and safeguards, run a consent system and act promptly on notice, and gives platforms one if they keep takedown procedures and act in good faith; either can raise it before trial, and a platform that wins on it is awarded its attorney fees;
  • requires a plaintiff to plead “with particularity” why the company doesn’t qualify for the safe harbor; a second failed complaint is dismissed with prejudice and the company is awarded its fees; and
  • creates the Digital Content Provenance Standards Act: large platforms must show and keep content-origin data, camera makers must embed it (for devices sold from 2028), and AI providers with over 1 million monthly users must label AI-made images, video and audio, enforced by the Division of Consumer Protection with fines up to $2,500 per violation.

How the final version differs from the original. Per the compare document (original bill against the second substitute), the original’s civil-liability sections included “statutory damages in an amount not less than $10,000 and not more than $100,000 for each violation” against generation services and $5,000 to $50,000 against platforms; those are gone. The original also had a separate “actual knowledge” clause for platforms and a rebuttable presumption after a missed takedown; the enacted version defines platform liability mainly by notice and takedown. File-sharing platforms were dropped from the definition of a large online platform, a section on licensing AI systems to third parties was removed, and the start date moved from May 6, 2026 to Jan. 1, 2027. The safe-harbor and pleading-standard sections are not among the original’s sections. Which substitute, the House’s first or the second, made each change was not determined; the compare to the first substitute was not read. The enacted law keeps that a plaintiff need not prove actual damages to win an order stopping the distribution and removing the images, and that a plaintiff who wins recovers attorney fees.

Graded Topic by Topic

IN PLAIN WORDSEach topic gets a grade, A (best) to F (worst), on five questions: Does it check power? Can the public see it? Is it clear who pays? What does it do to air, water and land? Do the people affected get a real say? N/A means the question doesn’t apply.

How to read these: grades judge only what has already happened in the filings as of Sept. 25, 2026. They judge the record, not the person. Scale: C means the record meets what the law requires. B or A means it goes beyond that in a way the record shows. D means required information is there but the key part is vague or bundled, or a public check is weakened. F means required information is missing, or a public check is removed with nothing in its place.

How the grades work

Every topic is graded on the same five questions, written once for this site and used on every page:

  • Power: Does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?
  • Transparency: Can the public actually see the process: posted hearings, itemized financials, disclosed conflicts, regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?
  • Financial accountability: Who actually pays, who’s protected from paying, and is that structure disclosed plainly or buried in a bundled figure?
  • Environmental impact: What’s the effect on water, wetlands and wildlife, and does the process treat that as a real constraint or a box to check?
  • Community impact: Who bears the cost or holds a historical or cultural stake in this decision, tribal or otherwise, and did the process give them real standing to be heard, or was their interest treated as a formality?

The scale: A goes well beyond what the law requires, in ways the record shows. B goes beyond the requirement. C meets what the law requires, and no more. D has the required information, but the key part is vague or lumped into a catch-all. F is missing required information, or removes a public check with nothing in its place. N/A means the question doesn’t fit the topic.

Ground rules: each topic is graded on its own, not averaged into one score for the person. Grades judge the record, not the person’s reputation or other conduct. Where how something was built and how it turned out differ, both are noted. Meeting the legal minimum earns a C, not a B.
