Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.

No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.

Where this judge serves

Who votes on this judge: voters in Weber County (courts in Roy). A Yes or No vote on a judge only appears on ballots in the area the judge serves.

Named in December 2016 by the Weber County Nominating Commission to fill the Roy City Justice Court seat left by Judge R. Scott Waterfall, conditional on new-judge orientation and Judicial Council certification. Before the bench he was Roy City’s assistant city attorney and prosecutor.

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Judge Nelson fails to meet the minimum performance standards for legal ability, integrity and judicial temperament, and procedural fairness.

Survey Respondent Recommendation:43% of survey respondents recommend Judge Nelson for retention, which is well below his justice court peers.

Performance Measures: Survey scores are analyzed for statistical significance to determine if the judge scored well above, above, consistent with, below, or well below their justice court peers. Well below peers: Legal Ability, Integrity & Judicial Temperament, Administrative Skills, Procedural Fairness

Respondent and Observer Feedback: Many respondents express strong concerns about Judge Nelson, but the courtroom observers are positive.

Survey results show problems with his temperament, and several survey comments describe him as impatient, demeaning, and harsh in tone. As one respondent puts it, the judge “indulges in extreme rhetoric and lecturing.”

Several respondents criticize his lack of impartiality and say that he is temperamental.

Survey results also point out that respondents have little confidence in his legal skills and his ability to promote trust and confidence.

A few respondents describe the judge as fair and passionate, and they, along with observers, say that he holds defendants accountable.

Observers appreciate the judge’s professionalism and consistency, and all four report confidence that they would be treated fairly if they were to appear in Judge Nelson’s court.

Judge Response: I respect the evaluation process and I’m happy to receive feedback. It has helped me grow as a judge and person. Independent Court Observers were universally positive about my courtroom abilities. They unanimously find me to be professional and consistent, and reported with confidence that they would be treated fairly in my courtroom. Judicial philosophy: Once there’s a guilty finding, the focus turns to solving problems and addressing patterns of unsafe behavior. I invite you to form your own opinion by observing me in court in person or virtually.

Courtroom observers, overall

  • Together, the four observers were positive about Judge Nelson.
  • All observers reported that if they appeared before Judge Nelson they would be treated fairly.

What observers agreed on

  • Observers variously reported that Judge Nelson managed the proceedings and kept them on track, showing an “impressive awareness” of case details. He presented as calm, professional, and firm, and he showed appreciation to participants and “genuine respect for the process and the people involved.” He worked intentionally and carefully, taking time with “complex cases” and verifying “those with suspended drivers licenses had someone to drive them home.” The judge explained information in “plain language” and made sure “defendants fully understood their options and the consequences of their decisions.”
  • All observers particularly highlighted the judge’s impartial actions. One reported that the judge reminded defendants that the prosecuting attorney was not their attorney, and another observed him assuring participants that they are innocent until proven guilty. All described the judge as consistent, and all found that he held defendants accountable for their actions. One noted that his “sentencing decisions were strict but consistent,” and another recorded that he wanted people to “think about the potential consequences of their actions.”

Other observations

  • Two observers spoke positively about the judge’s listening skills.
  • Two observers felt the judge demonstrated concern for people’s needs. One stated that he delayed a defendant’s fine payment “to give the man time to get a job,” while the other found him to be “[empathetic]” in his“concern for defendants.”

Source: Weber County 2020 General Election official summary report, Nov. 17, 2020 (pages 10–12), read from the report’s text layout.

Judge Nelson’s last JPEC report, in 2020, was a mid-level evaluation: 45 short interviews with court staff, attorneys, litigants and their families outside his courtroom, with no attorney survey. That report recommended retention by a 13–0 vote, found he passed 5 of 5 standards, and said nearly all respondents were positive. Three 2020 respondents raised concerns, one saying certain charges “push his buttons.”

The 2026 report is a full evaluation, with a survey of attorneys and court staff (42 completed out of 142 sampled) plus four courtroom observers. Because the two years used different methods, the 2020 and 2026 results do not measure the same things.

The 2020 report also notes he was appointed to the Harrisville City Justice Court in December 2019 and was the presiding judge for the Second District’s justice courts (Weber, Davis and Morgan counties).

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Trent Nelson was appointed to the Roy/Weber County Justice Court in February 2017, serving the communities of Roy City, unincorporated Weber County, West Haven City, and Hooper City. He and his wife have six children. Judge Nelson is a trained domestic mediator and has a Bachelor of Arts in Economics from Weber State University, a law degree from Brigham Young University, and an MBA from Utah State University. Prior to the bench, he was the prosecutor for Roy City, and had a private practice focused on juvenile law, family law, and estate planning, frequently serving as a Guardian ad Litem. He has been a member of the Utah Supreme Court’s Advisory Committee for Rules of Professional Conduct for over fifteen years. He has also served on Utah’s Judicial Ethics Advisory Committee, the Special Education Panel for the Utah Department of Education, DCFS’ Child Welfare Improvement Council, and as a member of Weber County’s committees on Disproportionate Minority Contact and Juvenile Substance Abuse Prevention.
