Where the records are, how to open them, and what to look for

Oct. 17, 2026 · Weber County · Public records only, no endorsements

What Shauna said on this slide

Thank you for having me. Today is not about who to vote for. It's about how to look, so you can check any candidate yourself, in any race, in any year. Everything I show you is free, public, and needs no special training. There's a downloadable guide with every link at the end.

Almost nobody reads them.

What Shauna said on this slide

Ads tell you what a candidate wants you to know. These records tell you who is paying, what they own, and what they have done. The state and county post them as filed. Anyone can compare them with other public records. That's what we're going to do today.

5

Weber County races on the 2026 ballot with only one candidate

Commission Seat B · Assessor · Attorney · Clerk/Auditor · Sheriff

Those were settled at the convention and the June primary. Research matters most early, and it still matters for the person who will hold the office.

What Shauna said on this slide

Look at the bottom half of your ballot. Five county offices have one name. Commission Seat B had a primary in June: Jon Beesley beat the sitting commissioner, Sharon Bolos. By the time most people paid attention, the choice was made. So the best time to research is before the convention and primary. But even an unopposed candidate will hold that office for four years, so their records are still worth reading.

Slide 4 of 22

Ground rules before you start

Records, not rumors

A Facebook post tells you where to look, not what is true.

Write down where you found it

Save the link, the report's name and the date.

Not found is not "not there"

Say where you looked. A missing record proves nothing.

Look at the actual page

A scanned form read by a computer can look blank when it isn't.

Plain, neutral words

"Reported," "listed," "did not list." Leave verdicts to judges.

Same test for everyone

Every candidate, every party, checked the same way.

What Shauna said on this slide

These are the rules I hold myself to. The scanned-form one matters: many county filings are pictures of paper. If a computer reads one, it can come back looking empty. Before you say anything is missing, open it and read it with your own eyes. And use plain words: say what the record shows. I'm not a judge, and neither are you.

Slide 6 of 22

Step 1

See your own ballot

vote.utah.gov

  1. Click "Learn about candidates & issues"
  2. Type your home address
  3. Write down every race and every name

Why start here

Weber County is split across several House districts and two congressional districts. Your neighbor's ballot may not match yours.

Also on vote.utah.gov: ballot tracking, judges on the ballot, and the voter pamphlet for Amendments A and B

What Shauna said on this slide

That list of names is your research list. I make it first, then decide which races to dig into.

What Shauna said on this slide

On the Weber County site, click Candidates, then Financial Disclosures. Each candidate's reports are listed by name with a link for each one. Older years are at the bottom of the same page. State law, section 17-70-304, says a county candidate's conflict form must be filed with their declaration and posted on the county website. If you can't find one, ask the elections office.

Slide 8 of 22

Step 3

Read the conflict-of-interest form

What the state form asks

  • Employer and job
  • Businesses they own or run
  • Income sources of $5,000 or more
  • Stocks and bonds of $5,000 or more
  • Board seats, paid or unpaid
  • Spouse's employer

Weber County example

Rep. Katy Hall's January 2026 form listed her company, Tava Town LLC.

Her July 2026 form answered "n/a" on owned businesses.

Putting two forms side by side is how that showed up.

Find it: disclosures.utah.gov → Search by Name. Source for example: Hall's filed forms, as reported in the Hive's HD-11 case file

What Shauna said on this slide

This is the most useful record, and the shortest. Write down every business, board and employer on it. You'll check each one in the next step. Keep in mind: some sections are optional, so a blank line isn't automatically a rule broken. The form asks about the year before. And it never asks about relatives outside the home, so family ties won't show up here. Legislators file new forms each year, so compare the newest with older ones.

Slide 9 of 22

Step 4

Check the form against the business registry

businessregistration.utah.gov

Search a person: Registered Principal Search shows every company where they're an officer, manager, director or agent.

Search a company: open it, then click "Access Principal Information."

Weber County example

For SD-5 candidate Jill Koford, the registry shows three roles not on her form: two boards and an LLC.

Common names bring up strangers. Match the middle initial and address.

Source for example: Utah Division of Corporations records and Koford's Jan. 10, 2026 form, as reported in the Hive's SD-5 case file

What Shauna said on this slide

The three roles: director of North Fork Meadows Owners Association, manager of Scoots Industries LLC, and director of Ogden Regional Airport Association. Speaker Mike Schultz's 2026 form answers "None" to the board question, while the registry lists him as director of one homeowners association and officer of another. Unpaid boards matter too. I search every candidate's name, then every company on their form and their donor list. Also check their campaign site and bio: candidates often list their boards when they're campaigning.

Slide 10 of 22

Big tip

OpenCorporates: companies outside Utah

opencorporates.com

One search across 140+ government registries. Many vendors and developers are registered in Delaware, Nevada or Wyoming, not Utah.

Officers searches a person's name across every registry at once.

Then cite the state

OpenCorporates copies government records. Click through to the state's own registry page, and cite that.

Use it when a company on a form or donor list doesn't show up in businessregistration.utah.gov

What Shauna said on this slide

When a company doesn't show up in Utah's registry, this is where I go next. Searching a person's name under Officers has turned up companies tied to the same home address that I wouldn't have found one state at a time.

Slide 11 of 22

Step 5

Follow the money

disclosures.utah.gov

One candidate: Search by Name → open their folder → open each report.

Everyone at once: Advanced Search → pick Entity Type and Report Year → Find → click the year under "Download Data by Year."

County candidates: weberelections.gov/financialdisclosures. Most are scanned PDFs.

What to look at

  • People or organizations?
  • Donors who live in the district
  • Timing of big gifts
  • Late, deleted, amended entries
  • Candidate's own money
  • Who gets paid
What Shauna said on this slide

The Advanced Search download is my favorite trick. It shows what every PAC reported paying out that year, so you can find money a candidate received even before their own report is posted. It also shows everything one donor gave to everyone. I sort donors into groups: individual people, caucus and leadership PACs, other organizations, party help, and the candidate's own money.

Slide 12 of 22

The 31-day check

84

days between when a gift was received and when it first appeared

HD-7: a $500 gift to Rep. Ryan Wilcox dated July 8, 2026 first appears in his filings on Sept. 30. Utah requires each contribution to be reported within 31 days.

Source: Wilcox's own filings on disclosures.utah.gov, as reported in the Hive's HD-7 case file · 31-day rule: disclosures.utah.gov home page

What Shauna said on this slide

This one takes no special skill. Every entry has a date received. Compare it with the report it first shows up on. The case file makes no finding about penalties; it just shows the two dates. Also: in the 30 days before the general election, a candidate in a contested race has to report each contribution within seven business days. We're in that window now.

Step 6

Look behind the PACs

A PAC's name says nothing about who is behind it. Every PAC files its own reports on disclosures.utah.gov: officers, donors, and who it gives to.

House Speaker Leadership PAC

UHREC

NUPAC

Same treasurer on all three: Kyle Palmer. His firm, Irish Elk LLC, is paid by these PACs and by candidates.

Source: PAC filings on disclosures.utah.gov, as reported in the Hive's Weber County voter guide. Lobbyists: lobbyist.utah.gov

What Shauna said on this slide

These three show up again and again in Weber County races. Giving to candidates is legal, and paying a treasurer's firm is permitted under current law. The voter's question is: who funds these PACs, and what does a network that funds many candidates at once expect from them? For lobbyists, lobbyist.utah.gov has "Lobbyists by Organization" to see who a company or a city hires.

Slide 14 of 22

Big tip

The Meta Ad Library: see the ads and who paid

facebook.com/ads/library

  1. Location: United States
  2. Category: Issues, elections or politics
  3. Search a candidate, PAC or keyword
  4. Filters → show active and inactive ads

Each political ad shows

  • "Paid for by"
  • Spending and views, as ranges
  • Who saw it: age, gender, state
  • Dates it ran

Spending by state: the Ad Library Report shows the top advertisers in Utah. Not all are political, so check each name.

Political ads stay in the library for seven years. Facebook and Instagram only; TV, radio and mail aren't in it.

What Shauna said on this slide

Campaign reports show a payment to an ad firm. This shows the ads themselves. Look up the "Paid for by" name on disclosures.utah.gov to see who's behind it. Watch for ads about a candidate run by someone other than their campaign. Spending is a range, so never quote it as an exact amount: say "Meta's Ad Library lists between X and Y." And a keyword search also finds ads that just mention a name, so check the "Paid for by" line before counting it.

Slide 15 of 22

Step 7

Check the record, not the promise

le.utah.gov

Find your legislators. Open a bill to see every version and every vote. Compare the first version with the one that passed.

utah.gov/pmn

Agendas and minutes for cities, counties, school boards and districts. Who voted how, and who disclosed a conflict.

Their own words

Campaign site, interviews, forums. Treat each claim as a claim until it matches a bill, vote or minutes.

What Shauna said on this slide

Campaign promises are easy. A record shows what someone did when it counted. On the Hive's pages, a candidate's description of their own record is labeled "in their own words" and sits right next to what the record shows. For Congress, fec.gov shows the money. For judges on the retention ballot, vote.utah.gov links to each judge's evaluation.

Slide 16 of 22

Big tip

The Wayback Machine: see what a page used to say

web.archive.org

  1. Paste the page's address
  2. Pick a year, then a marked day
  3. Write down the date on the copy

Save Page Now keeps a dated copy of a page before it changes.

Example

Election Hive's website once ran a bio page for Dan Hemmert, now MIDA's executive director.

The page has since been taken down. The saved copy still shows it.

Not every page was saved. No copy doesn't mean the page never existed.

What Shauna said on this slide

Websites change. Bios get rewritten, board lists get updated, pages come down. The Wayback Machine keeps saved copies, so you can compare the old and new versions the same way you'd compare two disclosure forms. A saved copy shows what the page said on that date, not why it changed. Before you share something you found on a website, save it yourself with Save Page Now.

Use them together: a company that gives to a candidate may also get federal money. A nonprofit's 990 lists its officers and pay.

What Shauna said on this slide

For U.S. House races, start at fec.gov: type the name and you'll see totals, how much came from individuals versus PACs, and each donor. A candidate only has to register with the FEC after raising or spending $5,000, so tiny campaigns may not be there. The Senate site asks you to check a box agreeing to its use rules before you search. For the IRS search, apps.irs.gov/app/eos, search by the organization's name or its EIN.

Slide 18 of 22

Common traps

What you seeWhat it may really mean
A blank-looking formA scanned page. Read it with your eyes.
"Campaign Expense"The public report shows the category; a typed description may not show.
A donor with the same nameMaybe a stranger. Match address and middle initial.
"Roost," "Roost Co."One vendor can be filed several ways. Search the short name.
Dates a day apartNormal filing lag between giver and receiver.
An unknown gift of $50 or lessAllowed by Utah Code 20A-11-301.
What Shauna said on this slide

One candidate, Angela Choberka, pointed out publicly that she typed descriptions for her expenses and they don't show on the state's public report. So never say a candidate "gave no description." Say the public report lists it as "Campaign Expense." Also: the address column on a donor list is the donor's address, not the candidate's. And note the date you looked at everything, because pages change.

One scale for everyone

A

Goes well beyond what the law requires

B

Goes beyond the requirement

C

Meets the law, and no more

D

There, but vague, lumped together or late

F

Required information missing

Five questions

Power · Transparency · Financial accountability · Environmental impact · Community impact

Campaign money

Graded on the share from individual people: A = 90%+, B = 67–89%, C = 34–66%, D = 10–33%, F = under 10%.

What Shauna said on this slide

You don't need a grading system to research a candidate. But if you want to compare fairly, use one scale for everyone, whatever their party. A C means the record meets what Utah law requires. B or A means they went beyond it. A grade can change: an amended form or report that adds what was missing changes the grade. Party help and a candidate's own money are shown but not counted in the money grade.

Sources: Lt. Governor's 2026 Financial Disclosure Deadlines (disclosures.utah.gov/File/308); FEC 2026 reporting dates. Weber County candidates: ask 801-399-8034

What Shauna said on this slide

Deadlines tell you when new information will show up, and whether a report came in on time. The state General Report covers Sept. 26 to Oct. 22 and is due Oct. 27, a week before Election Day. Weber County candidates file Convention, Pre-Primary, Primary, Pre-General and Final reports with the county, and the county says failing to file can disqualify a candidate. Put the deadlines on your calendar and check the reports the day after.
