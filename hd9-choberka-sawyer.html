What he promised vs. what he's sponsored

D · Promises Not Kept ↓ full grade breakdown in Bill Grades below

Sawyer's 2024 campaign named housing his explicit top priority. In a Salt Lake Tribune commentary, he wrote he'd "fight for policies that encourage responsible development and remove unnecessary regulations that drive up the cost of building homes," and told the Standard-Examiner directly: "My top priority will be lowering the cost of housing." He also pledged to prioritize education — properly funding schools, raising teacher pay, cutting administrative burden, expanding local control.

His full sponsored-bill record across two sessions shows he chief-sponsored one bill that shares "real estate" language with his campaign promise — but on closer reading, it isn't housing policy.

Struck via enacting-clause removal 3/6/26 (same night/mechanism as HB 100 and HB 267 below). Never passed.

Bill grades

This isn't a personal opinion of the bill. Each grade below asks two questions, applied the same way to every bill in this series — not whether any one person likes the outcome: 1. Power — does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over? 2. Transparency — does it add or remove what the public can actually see about the process, regardless of who ends up holding the final decision? Design and outcome are graded separately because a bill can be structurally sound and still fail to become law, or vice versa; collapsing the two into one score would hide which one actually happened. Graded here: his five chief-sponsored bills only — not the ones he merely floor-sponsored or cosponsored for other legislators, which wouldn't fairly reflect his own authorship.

▸ HB 493 Consumer Products Amendments B+ · Good

CORRECTED against the actual bill text (comparing HB0493 to its substitute, HB0493S01): this is not a shelf-pricing law, as earlier described here. It does two distinct things — establishes an escalating civil-penalty schedule for violations of the Dept. of Agriculture and Food's weights-and-measures program (commercial scales, pumps, packaged goods), and creates a new requirement that menstrual product manufacturers disclose on packaging any intentionally-added ingredient, specifically naming five chemicals if present: lead, cadmium, arsenic, dioxin (TCDD), and PFAS. Senate Sponsor: Ann Millner — the same Sen. Millner already documented elsewhere in this project as NUPAC's co-founder and Koford's Senate predecessor. Cosponsors: Kristen Chevrier and Candice B. Pierucci.

PowerNeutral — doesn't shift decision-making authority toward or away from the public on either provision; both are enforcement/disclosure mechanisms, not governance structures.
TransparencyPositive — the menstrual-product labeling requirement is a genuine consumer right-to-know improvement, giving the public visibility into product chemical content it didn't have before. The weights-and-measures penalty schedule doesn't change what the public can see, only the cost of getting caught violating an existing rule.

Design: B+. A modest, genuine consumer-protection measure with no structural downside found — the grade itself is unchanged by this correction, only the description of what earns it.

Did it change between introduction and passage? No bait-and-switch here — the substance was consistent from the introduced bill through to the enrolled law. The only "correction" involved was this page's own earlier wrong description (shelf-pricing), not a change in the bill itself.

Who benefits: Diffuse public benefit — consumers of menstrual products gain a genuine right-to-know on chemical content; no single named company gains or loses from either provision.

Outcome: B+ (signed). Design and outcome match here — it became law as written, effective 5/6/26, with the labeling requirement itself taking effect 12/1/27.

▸ HB 395 Real Estate Transaction Amendments C · Mixed
PowerMixed — addresses a real wire-fraud/cybercrime problem, but does it by requiring every real estate closing to route through ONE state-sanctioned private company, selected and regulated by a new 5-member board. That's a market-choice question as much as a security one: license holders lose the ability to pick their own transaction-communication vendor, and one company becomes the mandatory gatekeeper for the entire industry.
TransparencyNot established from what's on file whether the new board's vendor-selection and fee-approval process would be conducted in public hearings or behind closed doors — a genuine open question, not assumed either way.

Design: C. A legitimate problem addressed through a single-winner structure worth watching, not a clean transparency or consumer-choice win.

How it died, confirmed: Introduced 1/28/26, referred to House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee 2/3/26, that committee "recommends returned to Rules" 3/4/26, then struck via enacting-clause removal at 11:59 PM on 3/6/26 — the same night and mechanism, and the identical committee-to-Rules pipeline, already documented elsewhere on this page killing H.B. 100, plus H.B. 267, H.B. 422, and H.J.R. 7 in this project's broader bait-and-switch tracker.

Did it change between introduction and its death? Not established — only the final (pre-death) text is on file here, not an earlier version to compare it against. Unlike HB 236 elsewhere on this page, no bait-and-switch pattern is confirmed for this bill; it may simply have read this way from the start.

What would have made it good for the collective: Keep the anti-wire-fraud goal, but let licensed title/escrow/real-estate professionals choose among multiple certified transaction-network vendors instead of one exclusive company, and require the new board's vendor-selection and fee-approval meetings to be open to the public.

Who benefits: Not established — the bill died before the new 5-member board ever selected the "one exclusive private company," so no specific vendor was ever named as the winner. If it had passed, that company would have been the clear concentrated beneficiary; for now this sits in industry-generally, no-company-named territory.

Outcome: N/A — struck via enacting-clause removal, 3/6/26, never passed. No real-world effect to grade; the design concerns above never had to be tested.

Also cosponsored (not graded above — not his own bill)

The attack ads run against her, and who paid for them

Preserving Utah Values PAC ran attack ads against Choberka in June 2026 — a Wisconsin-registered PAC (officers Thomas and Timothy Datwyler, Hudson, WI), created 10/8/2024. Its only documented contribution, $120,000, came directly from Mike Schultz personally (11/30/2024) — the same House Speaker whose own leadership PAC and party network fund a large share of Sawyer's own campaign, documented earlier on this page. The money was spent almost entirely on a single vendor, G2 Consulting ($110,535).

The PAC filed a dissolution "Final Report" on 6/20/2025 — yet a separate report filed the same day shows $2,914.57 in expenditures, a contradiction in its own paperwork. The ads themselves ran under "Paid for by Preserving Utah Values Inc," and zero 2026 disclosures have been filed anywhere in Utah's campaign finance system for the period the ads actually ran — a documented compliance gap, not an assumption. The Salt Lake Tribune independently identified Schultz as the funder behind this network, using his own photo alongside the story.*

Choberka is one of at least three candidates targeted by this same PAC — Scott Stephenson (HD-44) was targeted the same year, 2026, alongside her; Rosemary Lesser was targeted earlier, during her 2024 race, which she lost by roughly 300 votes. (Note: a similarly-named but entirely separate group, "Protect Utah Values PAC," is Utah-registered and funded by a different individual — not to be confused with this one.)

*The Tribune's naming of Schultz was tied specifically to the 2024 Lesser race.
