Five Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSA House member who won a hard primary faces a first-time candidate with no reported money. The House member’s record is mostly about technology and privacy.

  1. Fiefia raised $161,780 in new money through June 25, plus a $31,504.12 transfer from his own House campaign. About 58% came from individuals. His largest checks: ProDough $20,000, and $10,000 each from Encode AI Corporation (Washington, D.C.), Elk Ridge Management and Jeremy Andrus (Las Vegas).
  2. He spent $134,532.83 through the primary, including $24,000 to the Sandy Expo Center and $12,444 in Utah Republican Party postage.
  3. Three of his six 2026 bills became law. His AI Transparency Act (H.B. 286) died on the last night of the session, after a White House letter called it “unfixable.”
  4. Two tech-privacy laws he worked on ended up narrower. H.B. 408, as signed, delays Utah’s 2025 data-portability law by a year (a change made in the Senate) and trims it. S.B. 267, which he carried in the House, went from binding student-privacy rules to a study.
  5. Anderson has no contributions or spending on file, per a search of the state’s disclosure site on Sept. 24. His form lists one job: special loans analyst at the Utah Housing Corporation.

FIEFIA’S 2026 BILLS

Three Signed, Two AI Bills Died

IN PLAIN WORDSFiefia’s bills focus on technology, privacy and children. Three of his own became law. His two AI bills died on the last night. Two bills he carried were narrowed before passing.

From the Legislature’s bill list for Doug Fiefia, 2026 General Session, and bill texts, compare documents, committee minutes and roll calls on le.utah.gov.

About H.B. 291 (primary sponsor Rep. Candice Pierucci): as signed, it lowers the ownership share that makes a company a “restricted foreign entity” from 51% to 25%, has the Department of Public Safety send a letter to parties it suspects, gives them 30 days to answer, and allows a $500-a-day fine for not answering. Effective May 6, 2026. Source: H.B. 291 enrolled text, le.utah.gov.

How H.B. 408 changed, step by step

  • Feb. 24: House committee passes Substitute 1, 7–2. Fiefia, a committee member, votes yes; Reps. Hansen and Hawkins vote no.
  • March 2: House passes it 66–1 (Hansen no).
  • March 3: In the Senate committee, Fiefia presents it with Zach Boyd, director of the state Office of Artificial Intelligence. A motion to skip it fails 1–3; Sen. Karen Kwan moves to pass it, and it passes 3–1 (draft minutes).
  • Then: The Senate adopts Substitute 3, which among other changes moves the bill’s effective date from 2026 to 2027. As signed, the law pushes the 2025 data-portability law’s start to July 1, 2027, drops the requirement to offer competitors “a functionally equivalent version” of internal interfaces, and narrows the data users can take, while adding consent and disclosure rules. Which of those changes came from the Senate beyond the date wasn’t determined.
  • March 5: The House accepts the Senate version 70–1, with Fiefia voting yes.
Changes made and voted on in the open

S.B. 267: from rules to a study

As introduced by Sen. Kirk Cullimore, S.B. 267 would have required every school software vendor to sign a statewide student-privacy contract, prove its software helps students learn, and give up addictive design features, most biometric tracking, and using student data to train commercial AI. It included audits of every school district and a parent complaint process backed by the Attorney General. The version Fiefia carried in the House, and that became law, asks the State Board of Education to study those same topics and post guidance by July 1, 2027. It sets no requirements and no enforcement.

THE AI BILL

H.B. 286: Passed Committee 8–0, Never Got a Floor Vote

IN PLAIN WORDSFiefia’s biggest bill would have required the largest AI companies to publish safety plans and protected whistleblowers. After the White House opposed it, it died without a final vote.

  • Passed the House Economic Development committee 8–0 on Jan. 27 (two members absent).
  • On Feb. 12, the White House Office of Intergovernmental Affairs wrote to Senate Majority Leader Kirk Cullimore: “We are categorically opposed to Utah HB 286 and view it as an unfixable bill that goes against the Administration’s AI Agenda.”
  • After being circled on the floor, it was sent back to the Rules Committee on March 3. Its enacting clause was struck at 11:59 p.m. on March 6, the last night, and it was filed with no recorded floor vote.
  • Fiefia to the Associated Press (April 19): “The Trump administration is, ‘We want zero regulations on AI.’ I think that’s wrong.” Sen. Dan McCay, his primary opponent, said the bill would have “driven Utah out of the AI innovation business.”

Full case file: H.B. 286, AI Transparency Act (Bill-Tracker).
