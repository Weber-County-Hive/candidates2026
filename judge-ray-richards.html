Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge and JPEC staff interview people leaving court. The judge must pass every minimum standard (5 of 5).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Appointed in 2012, Judge Ray R. Richards received mostly positive reviews from the observer and the respondents. He presented as an approachable, friendly judge who demonstrated sensitivity to court participants. The judge ensured the understanding of participants by providing complete explanations, and he gave them ample time to express themselves. This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

*This report was completed in 2022, when COVID-19 limited JPEC’s ability to gather data for justice court judges with mid-sized caseloads. JPEC evaluates each judge once every six years, and the same report may be used when a judge serves in multiple courts.

How JPEC gathered this report

Judge Ray Richards’s report consists of a two-hour observation conducted on two different days via WebEx videoconferencing. It also consists of four interviews conducted via telephone and email. The interview sample consists of court staff and attorneys. Court staff may include clerks, bailiffs, interpreters, and service providers. The Covid-19 pandemic constrained JPEC’s ability to gather data to evaluate justice court judges with mid-sized caseloads. Commission conclusions for this judge are based on less data than in years prior.

Overall

Judge Richards received mostly positive reviews from the observer and the respondents. He presented as an approachable, friendly judge who demonstrated sensitivity to court participants. The judge ensured the understanding of participants by providing complete explanations, and he gave them ample time to express themselves.

What most people said

The observer reported that Judge Richards exhibited skillful interpersonal skills and engaged people in a respectful, considerate manner. The judge expressed appreciation consistently to all parties, saying, “I appreciate you appearing before me,” or, “Have a good rest of your day. Thank you for linking in with me.” At one point during a hearing, a litigant, who was waiting for another proceeding, thought that the judge was talking to her, so she verbally responded. The judge demonstrated patience and responded politely, informing her that he was speaking to somebody involved in the current hearing. Whenever a defense attorney asked for a continuance on a case, the judge always made sure that litigants were comfortable with the request. In a case involving a serious alcohol charge, he spoke to the litigant in a serious but compassionate tone, “Mr. [X], I want you to do well. I do. I don’t want you to be in trouble.”

The observer recorded that Judge Richards explained information thoroughly and prioritized participants’ voices in interactions. His explanations were helpful, in terms of explaining the positives and negatives of a particular situation, case, or process. In one matter, he explained fully what a litigant needed to do to avoid further problems with alcohol. The judge allowed participants to speak about their situations and points of view, in one case, asking a litigant what he wanted to do and how he wanted to move forward. In another, he asked, “What happened here, Mr. [X]?” The judge also ensured that the defense attorney and prosecutor share all that they need to share before proceeding.

In one instance, the observer noted that the judge appeared to be less careful. In this case, after having listened to the various parties’ input before a litigant’s sentencing, the judge asked the prosecutor if he had anything more to add. The prosecutor made a short response, but he was muted, so his voice was not audibly heard in the hearing. The judge did not verify what was said and just moved on.

Three of the four respondents found that Judge Richards interacts respectfully with court participants and is sensitive to their situations. One indicated that he is known for “treating people fairly and with respect” and added that he “always goes the extra mile to assist [participants] in any way he can.” Another found the judge to be “accommodating,” while yet another acknowledged that the judge considers the litigants’ age, education levels, and whatever else they may need when he sentences them.

One respondent described the judge as “consistent,” indicating that he is “very fair across the board.”

One respondent stated that the judge can sometimes take “a lot of time explaining and going over things with people…but that’s not necessarily a bad thing.”

One respondent said that “defendants and victims [come] out of court with Judge Richards feeling like they've been heard and been treated fairly.”

Comments that stood apart

One respondent felt that the judge “goes too far to discuss defendants’ rights…to the point where people start to question” their decisions, and he/she suggested that “it would work better to simply give a good explanation of their rights and let them make an adult decision.”

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Ray Robert Richards was appointed to the Vernal City Justice Court bench in 2012. Judge Richards is a 1996 social work graduate from Utah State University. Prior to his appointment, Judge Richards worked for 16 years for the Eighth District Juvenile Court; first as a probation officer and later as chief probation officer. He is a certified firefighter with the State of Utah and currently volunteers for both Vernal City and Uintah County.
