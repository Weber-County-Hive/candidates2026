How this guide works

This guide does two jobs. It shows what to look at when you choose a candidate, so you can check any race yourself. And it shows what the public record says about the Wasatch County races the Hive has reviewed.

  • Records, not rumors. Forms candidates filed, the state business registry, campaign finance reports, meeting records, hearing transcripts and candidates’ own public words. Tips and social media claims are leads, not facts.
  • Their words next to the record. When a candidate has explained something publicly, it is included and labeled as theirs.
  • Not found is not the same as not there. When a record hasn’t been located, the guide says where the Hive looked.
  • The deal, not the reputation. A good or bad reputation doesn’t answer the question. The record does.

Six things to look at

None of these steps takes special training. Each uses records that are public and free.

1. Read the conflict-of-interest form, then check it

Wasatch County’s one-page form asks the filer to describe “any relationships, transactions, positions you hold (volunteer or otherwise), or circumstances that you believe could contribute to a conflict of interest between Wasatch County and your personal interests, financial or otherwise.” The filer checks “no conflict of interest to report” or “the following conflict of interest to report.”

Check it against: the Utah business registry (businessregistration.utah.gov), the candidate’s own bio, website and posts, and the rosters posted by the boards themselves.

Keep in mind: “you believe” makes it a judgment call. A “no conflict” answer isn’t automatically a violation. Ask whether that judgment was reasonable for an office that votes on land use, contracts and funding.

Where: the county’s Conflict of Interest page and the Declaration of Candidacy packets on the Election page. Some forms are filed only inside the packet.

2. Follow the money

County candidates’ reports are on the county Election page; legislative reports are on disclosures.utah.gov; congressional reports are on fec.gov. Look at people vs. organizations, local vs. outside money, timing, and self-funding. Look up any PAC’s own report: its name tells you almost nothing, and some PACs are funded mostly by transfers from nonprofits that don’t name their donors.

3. Look for a record from before the campaign

Votes and minutes if they’ve held office, hearing comments, letters to the editor, past board service. For a first-time candidate, a hearing comment or a published letter may be the whole record, and that’s fine.

4. Map the boards and roles

Council members also sit on MIDA and its districts, the Wasatch Open Lands Board, special service districts, tourism boards and nonprofits. Which boards, who appointed them, do those boards come before the council, and will the candidate step aside on those votes?

5. Compare what they say with what the record shows

Treat a candidate’s description of their own record as a claim until it matches minutes, votes or filings.

6. Ask the five questions that matter to everyone

QuestionWhat to ask a candidate
PowerWould you add or remove a check on decisions the public has no say over?
TransparencyWill you disclose conflicts in writing and out loud before votes? Will hearings, budgets and contracts be itemized and posted?
Financial accountabilityOn tax-increment financing, public infrastructure districts and sole-source contracts: who pays, who is protected from paying, and will you show the numbers?
Environmental impactDo water, wetlands, open space and wildlife count as a real limit in your land-use votes?
Community impactWho bears the cost of growth, and do residents get real standing before decisions are made?
Cautions. Check the date on everything; board lists and websites go stale. A scanned form read by a computer can look blank when it isn’t, so look at the actual page before calling anything missing. Social media is a lead, not a source.

How the Hive grades

Every candidate and topic in the Hive’s case files is graded on the five questions above, using one scale. Grades judge the record, not the person. Meeting the legal minimum earns a C, not a B. Each topic is graded on its own; “Overall” averages only the questions that could be graded.

GradeWhat it means
AGoes well beyond what the law requires, in ways the record shows
BGoes beyond the requirement
CMeets what the law requires, and no more
DRequired information is there, but the key part is vague or lumped into a catch-all

A question about the form itself

State law (Utah Code §17-70-304) describes a candidate disclosure form divided into sections for each category, with space under each. The state form Luke Searle filed for HD-59 has about eight sections. The Wasatch County form is one question, two checkboxes and three blank lines. Whether it meets the state’s description is a question for the Clerk/Auditor’s office.

What state law requires

Utah Code §17-70-304 says the county clerk’s form for county and local school board candidates must have a separate section, with space to answer, for each item listed in §20A-11-1604(6):

  • Every employer, now and in the past year, with occupation and job title
  • Every business or entity the candidate owns or is an officer of, what it does, and their position
  • Every source of income of $5,000 or more
  • Stocks or bonds worth $5,000 or more
  • Every board seat and leadership role, and what the organization does
  • The spouse’s employment
  • Any unrelated adult living in the household, and that person’s job if it could be a conflict
  • Real property and any other conflict (optional)
  • Date, a statement that it is true, and signature

The same law says the statement is filed with the Declaration of Candidacy, that the clerk “may not accept a declaration of candidacy” until it receives a complete statement, and that the clerk posts it on the county website through the canvass. A “private party in interest” can sue to enforce it, and the court may award costs and attorney fees. The one exception: an incumbent seeking reelection who already filed this year’s annual statement and confirms in writing that it is current. The Wasatch County form never asks about income sources, stocks or bonds, last year’s employers, property or household members.

Sources: Utah Code §17-70-304 (effective Nov. 6, 2025) and §20A-11-1604(6), le.utah.gov.

Why the family questions matter

We used these two Weber County examples on purpose: they show real connections going unlisted because the disclosure forms don’t ask the questions that would let voters make educated decisions. Both come from the Hive’s published reporting. Both Katrina Gibson and Sharon Bolos lost their June 2026 primaries; the point is the form, which didn’t require any of these relationships to be listed.

The Gibson family

Katrina Gibson ran for Weber County Commission Seat A. Her form listed her husband’s employer as “N/A.” Kerry Gibson is a registered lobbyist whose active clients include Ogden City, Plain City, Morgan County and Wolf Creek Management, John Lewis’s company. Rep. Ryan Wilcox is Kerry’s nephew, per family obituaries. Utah Forward PAC, where Katrina is CFO and registered agent, paid Wilcox $3,000 on Jan. 21, 2026; Wilcox gave $500 to her campaign and endorsed her. The PAC paid Steven Gibson $1,000 as “Donations” (July 29, 2025); Kerry wished him “happy birthday cuz” in a Facebook post. It also paid $7,158 to Politix LLC, which Kerry and Katrina own.

The Bolos family

Commissioner Sharon Bolos, West Haven mayor from 2014 to 2021, has a brother, Bryan Arrington, whom state corporate records list as manager of Nilson Land Holdings LLC. Nilson Homes came before her West Haven council more than 11 times from 2015 to 2018 and before the County Commission in 2023, 2024 and 2025, with no disclosure of the family tie in the minutes. She has said he no longer works there in that capacity; her brother Jeffrey Arrington wrote publicly on Facebook that Bryan “does still work there.” Her daughter, Alexis Bolos Jackson, was elected to the Roy City Council in 2025 and sits on the Weber County RAMP committee, a seat the Commission appoints.
