Six Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSA long-serving senator whose campaign spends more than it takes in faces a first-time Forward Party candidate with almost no cash. His record centers on where Uintah Basin oil and gas money goes.

  1. Winterton’s campaign is spending more than it raises. From Jan. 1, 2025 through June 7, 2026 it took in $20,300.00 and spent $68,155.08. His 2026 Primary Report begins at negative $91,381.94 and ends at negative $95,804.74.
  2. Almost none of his money comes from individuals. One person gave $50. The Utah Republican Senate Campaign Committee gave $5,000; the rest came from businesses, PACs and associations, including energy, health care and banking groups.
  3. Most of his spending is travel, hotels and a credit card. $18,230.68 went to Little America Hotel and $15,259.03 to American Express, whose statements aren’t itemized in the filings. $12,000 went to Outcry, a Heber campaign firm, filed as “Constituent Services.”
  4. His bills decide where Basin oil and gas money goes. S.B. 107 (2023) sent above-trend severance tax to a statewide transportation fund instead of a Basin fund. S.B. 207 (2025) bans county oil and gas road fees with no end date, in exchange for a state tax that ends in 2029.
  5. His disclosure form lists no business ownership. Utah’s business registry lists him as a member or director of six active companies, each renewed within the past year, including R & L Storage, Winterton Trucking and W M Enterprises.
  6. McRae reports $180 in cash from three people, one of them herself. The Forward Party gave $6,696.94 in voter data. Her disclosure form lists four jobs, including Lyft driver, and says her husband hauls crude oil.

What state law says about card payments

Paying campaign costs with a credit card is not the issue. Reporting is. Utah Code 20A-11-101.5, in effect since 2014, defines a “transactional intermediary” as “a credit card company, a financial institution, or a money transfer service,” and says a report must reveal “the actual person or entity to whom the disbursement is ultimately made” and “may not merely list, disclose, or report the transactional intermediary.”

After a complaint about Senate President Stuart Adams’s reports, the Lieutenant Governor’s Office issued guidance, reported by Utah Political Watch on Jan. 3, 2025: listing “a credit card company (e.g. American Express) … is insufficient; the final payee of the good or service must be listed.” The office said the requirement “will not apply retroactively, but will be enforced moving forward.” The same guidance says listing yourself as the payee when reimbursing yourself is also insufficient.

Winterton’s reports list American Express as the payee for $15,259.03 across nine payments, all dated Jan. 2, 2025 or later. This page compares the filings with the law’s wording; whether a report complies is for the Lieutenant Governor’s Office to decide.

H.B. 330 (2026): a defense for permitted activity, changed in the Senate

H.B. 330 (2026): a defense for permitted activity, changed in the Senate

Winterton was Senate sponsor of Rep. Colin Jack’s H.B. 330, “Liability Limitations Amendments,” the House companion to the same pair’s S.B. 227. As signed, in its second substitute, it gives a person sued in a civil case an affirmative defense if the conduct, omission or condition that caused the harm was authorized or required by a statute, ordinance, administrative rule, “a permit, license, or order,” or another written instrument from a government entity, and that authorization was in effect at the time. The defense does not apply if the plaintiff proves the government entity itself has determined the person materially failed to comply, exceeded the authorization, or got it by fraud. It does not apply to product liability cases, does not remove the duty to use reasonable care, applies “regardless of the theory of liability,” and overrides common law that conflicts with it.

What changed from the first substitute: the first substitute said the defense would not apply when the harm was “proximately caused by negligent, reckless, fraudulent, or deceptive conduct in obtaining or carrying out the authorized activity,” “including a failure to disclose known material risks.” The second substitute removed that exception. In its place it keeps only fraud in getting the authorization, plus a line that the bill doesn’t remove the duty of reasonable care. The product exclusion was also shortened: the first substitute listed claims over a “defect, dangerous condition, or failure to warn” in a product, and the second says “a products liability action.”

Votes on record so far: the House Judiciary Committee recommended the original bill 7–0 on Feb. 2, 2026 (four absent). The House passed the first substitute, the version with the negligent, reckless or deceptive-conduct exception, 65–0 on Feb. 10. The second substitute, which removed that exception, was adopted in the Senate Economic Development and Workforce Services Committee on Feb. 17 (3–0 to substitute, 4–0 to recommend), and the Senate passed it 20–5 and then 17–7 on Feb. 25, with Winterton voting yes. The no votes: Sens. Blouin, Escamilla, Kwan, McKell, Pitcher, Plumb and Riebe. Among other senators with 2026 case files here, Buss (SD-11) voted yes, and Kwan (SD-12) and Plumb (SD-9) voted no. Graded Topic by Topic

IN PLAIN WORDSEach topic gets a grade, A (best) to F (worst), on five questions: Does it check power? Can the public see it? Is it clear who pays? What does it do to air, water and land? Do the people affected get a real say? N/A means the question doesn’t apply.

How to read these: grades judge only what has already happened in the filings as of Sept. 24, 2026. They judge the record, not the person. Scale: C means the record meets what the law requires. B or A means it goes beyond that in a way the record shows. D means required information is there but the key part is vague or bundled. F means required information is missing.

How the grades work

Every topic is graded on the same five questions, written once for this site and used on every page:

  • Power: Does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?
  • Transparency: Can the public actually see the process: posted hearings, itemized financials, disclosed conflicts, regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?
  • Financial accountability: Who actually pays, who’s protected from paying, and is that structure disclosed plainly or buried in a bundled figure?
  • Environmental impact: What’s the effect on water, wetlands and wildlife, and does the process treat that as a real constraint or a box to check?
  • Community impact: Who bears the cost or holds a historical or cultural stake in this decision, tribal or otherwise, and did the process give them real standing to be heard, or was their interest treated as a formality?

The scale: A goes well beyond what the law requires, in ways the record shows. B goes beyond the requirement. C meets what the law requires, and no more. D has the required information, but the key part is vague or lumped into a catch-all. F is missing required information, or removes a public check with nothing in its place. N/A means the question doesn’t fit the topic.

Ground rules: each topic is graded on its own, not averaged into one score for the person. Grades judge the record, not the person’s reputation or other conduct. Where how something was built and how it turned out differ, both are noted. Meeting the legal minimum earns a C, not a B.
