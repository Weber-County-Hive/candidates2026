Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

JPEC measure: Procedural Fairness. Passed every minimum standard JPEC applies to this kind of evaluation (5 of 5), including procedural fairness.
2. Follows the lawN/A

3. Temperament & integrityN/A
JPEC measure: Integrity & Judicial Temperament. Not surveyed; not counted.
4. Runs the court wellN/A
JPEC measure: Administrative Skills. Not surveyed; not counted.
5. Public recordN/A
H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.
OverallC
Average of the graded questions (A=4 … F=0; N/A left out): 2.0. A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge and JPEC staff interview people leaving court. The judge must pass every minimum standard (5 of 5).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Appointed in 2020, Judge Olds received positive reviews from the observer and all respondents. The judge presented as a courteous but firm judge who prioritized litigant understanding and considered litigant needs. He modeled consistent and neutral interactions and displayed a thorough manner in dispensing with all matters. This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

How JPEC gathered this report

Judge Olds' report consists of a two-hour observation conducted on three different days via WebEx videoconferencing. It also consists of 20 interviews conducted in-person and via email. The interview sample consists of litigants and their friends and family members, court staff, and attorneys. Court staff may include clerks, bailiffs, interpreters, and service providers.

Overall

Judge Olds' received positive reviews from the observer and all respondents. The judge presented as a courteous but firm judge who prioritized litigant understanding and considered litigant needs. He modeled consistent and neutral interactions and displayed a thorough manner in dispensing with all matters.

What most people said

The observer reported that the judge displayed a careful, dedicated approach to ensuring that litigants understood what was expected of them. He strived for clear communication and was very intentional in being understood by all parties. When one litigant struggled to follow the judge’s instructions, he slowed down and clearly enunciated his words so that there was no mistake as to what he was saying. He was diligent in making sure that litigants read the rights form and understood its content. He also made sure that parties had no questions and that litigants were thinking clearly and exercising proper judgment. When a young litigant questioned what thinking clearly and making his plea voluntarily meant, the judge paused and willingly talked through the meaning of these terms, demonstrating thoroughness. The judge explained the pre-trial conference process, how to go about paying fines, and went through several different options relating to one jail inmate’s situation.

The observer found that Judge Olds displayed a formal but respectful demeanor. He was firm and business-like and held parties accountable for their actions, but he was also flexible and compassionate to litigants’ various situations. He conveyed a calm demeanor, formally addressed participants by their titles, and wished them well when finishing matters. he commended one litigant for coming to court when she knew that she could not continue making payments according to the current terms, and he acted neutrally when assisting another in a domestic violence case. He exhibited sternness when litigants were not complying with court orders, and he emphasized consequences for not doing so. Yet, in most cases, he worked with them to ensure they could comply. In one matter, a litigant explained why he had not been making payments and requested to pay $20 a month. The judge told him that he normally would not allow that, but, in this case, he would do so. The judge also allowed participants to express themselves. To one litigant, he said, “Tell me what we need to do to get this behind you.” Upon listening to the response, he replied, “I get it.” After listening to another describe her difficult situation, he took a moment to think, showing that he was considering her input.

The observer noted that while the judge’s communication generally was stellar, he could work on communicating consistently during gaps and transitions between cases. Occasionally, he would pause in between certain cases and not communicate what he was doing, and, in one case, he just got up and left the bench when a few parties were still present online. In another matter, after a litigant replied to the judge that she had no further questions about the case, he looked at his computer and then just went on to the next case, without formally dismissing her case.

Interview Summary: Twelve of the 20 respondents commented favorably on Judge Olds' “respectful” and “professional” demeanor. Three described him as “very nice,” and two stated that he gave people chances to be better. One stated that he was “nonjudgmental,” and another indicated that the judge “puts people at ease.” Another appreciated that when entering court, the judge’s “good mood…radiates” the courtroom, creating a “comfortable and confident” presence.

Four people valued the judge’s consistency and neutral behavior. One said that “he treats everybody the same,” while another emphasized that he “doesn’t grant favorites” and “sticks to the statutes.” Another stated that the judge was “consistent with all expectations regarding sentencing and probation.”

Three commented positively on the judge’s communication skills. One reported that he was “direct,” while another said that he “does a good job of explaining things to people.” The third appreciated that the judge made sure litigants “understand the proceedings.”

Three admired the judge’s careful manner. Two described him as “thorough,” while another said that he operated in a manner “that would give people the most support” rather than just finishing quickly and moving on.

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Paul H. Olds was appointed to the Riverdale City Justice Court in October of 2019. He was subsequently appointed in 2021 to serve in the justice courts of Washington Terrace/Marriott Slaterville, Farr West, and Pleasant View. Judge Olds is a graduate of the University of Utah and the University of Nebraska College of Law. Prior to taking the bench, Judge Olds practiced as an attorney in Northern Utah for over 25 years.
