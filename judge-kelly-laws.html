Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

3. Temperament & integrityN/A
JPEC measure: Integrity & Judicial Temperament. Not surveyed; not counted.
4. Runs the court wellN/A
JPEC measure: Administrative Skills. Not surveyed; not counted.
5. Public recordN/A
H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.
OverallC
Average of the graded questions (A=4 … F=0; N/A left out): 2.0. A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge and JPEC staff interview people leaving court. The judge must pass every minimum standard (5 of 5).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Judge Laws received positive reviews from the observer and nearly all respondents. The judge presented as well-mannered and formal but caring. He prioritized informing and educating all litigants and worked carefully to be neutral and safeguard the rights of those in court. This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

How JPEC gathered this report

Judge Kelly Laws’s report consists of a two-hour observation conducted on two different days via Webex videoconferencing. It also consists of 13 interviews conducted in-person and via telephone and email. The interview sample includes litigants and their friends and family members, court staff, and attorneys. Court staff may include clerks, bailiffs, interpreters, and service providers.

Overall

Judge Laws received positive reviews from the observer and nearly all respondents. The judge presented as well-mannered and formal but caring. He prioritized informing and educating all litigants and worked carefully to be neutral and safeguard the rights of those in court.

What most people said

The observer reported that Judge Laws presented as a polite, conscientious judge who takes seriously the need to inform litigants, clarify information, and underscore their rights. Judge Laws exhibited a diligent manner in ensuring that litigants understood their charges, the instructions they received in court, and in answering their questions. He communicated clearly, explaining to one litigant why he was going to require the maximum fine in the case, and, on another matter, he took a moment to emphasize to the litigant that the charges were serious. He demonstrated consistency in ensuring that litigants knew their rights, saying at one point during the proceedings that the most important thing was to go over people’s rights and make sure people understood them. In one hearing, he sensed that a litigant was unsure as to how to proceed, so he told her that he didn’t want her to plead to anything under pressure and then made sure she qualified for a public defender.

According to the observer, Judge Laws conveyed a civil demeanor and behaved politely towards court participants. He routinely thanked participants, including bailiffs, attorneys, and litigants and wished them a good day. In a couple of instances, he showed consideration to litigants by asking them which of their names he should use to address them. When he had a difficult time pronouncing one name, he asked permission to use the other name. He demonstrated a willingness to hear input from participants before he made decisions. He also showed patience in a couple of cases where litigants failed to meet certain deadlines, and, though he held litigants accountable for their inaction, he appeared flexible in working with their circumstances so they could be successful.

Over half the respondents commented on the judge’s positive treatment of court participants. They variously found him to be gracious and professional in his interactions, with a couple describing him as kind, while another two respondents said he was patient. A few appreciated that the judge allowed people to express themselves, and some highlighted that he accommodated people’s needs while applying the law. One said, “He cares about folks” and “cares about their treatment,” while another shared that the judge “applies the law as it is written, but he does consider circumstance when sentencing.”

Several respondents spoke highly of the judge’s neutrality. They variously found him to be thorough and careful and willing to take time with cases. One said that he reviews “each case carefully,” while another stated, “He does his best to correct the course” when there’s a lack of clarity or confusion. A few respondents emphasized the judge’s impartial treatment of all participants. One shared that he “[treats] everyone equally,” and another noted that he treats litigants from out of state the same way he treats local litigants with whom he may be familiar.

Comments that stood apart

One respondent expressed varied criticism of the judge, indicating that he could improve his communication, improve his efficiency in court—particularly in calendar management, and provide more flexibility regarding in-person appearances of litigants in court.

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Kelly G. Laws was appointed to the Blanding City Justice Court in April 2023. Judge Laws is a lifetime resident of Blanding. His life has been full of hands-on experience. After attending Utah Technical College at Provo studying Diesel and Heavy Duty Mechanics, He worked for a minerals exploration company and soon became the Drilling superintendent over 6 drilling rigs. Later he operated heavy equipment building roads, dams, and highways. He retired from a Caterpillar dealership after 21 years of being a Field Service Technician troubleshooting and repairing some of the world's largest mining equipment to the smallest construction equipment while serving the residents of southeastern Utah. All while serving 18 years on the Blanding City council, 20 years on the San Juan County Transportation Special Service District. In 1987 Judge Laws purchased his family farm. In 2023 two of his sons joined him, they own and operate Two Cedars Angus LLC raising registered Angus breeding stock. Judge Laws is committed to unbiased justice for his community.
