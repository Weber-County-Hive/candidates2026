Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

JPEC measure: Administrative Skills. Not surveyed; not counted.
5. Public recordN/A
H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.
OverallC
Average of the graded questions (A=4 … F=0; N/A left out): 2.0. A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge and JPEC staff interview people leaving court. The judge must pass every minimum standard (5 of 5).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Appointed in 2015, Judge Brower received positive reviews from the observer and all respondents. He presented as highly professional, notably in his ability to communicate well with all parties in court and in a courteous demeanor. The judge also demonstrated thoroughness and neutrality and held others accountable. This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

How JPEC gathered this report

Judge Bower's report consists of a two-hour observation conducted on one day via WebEx videoconferencing. It also consists of 12 interviews conducted in-person and via telephone. The interview sample includes litigants and their friends and family members, court staff, and attorneys. Court staff may include clerks, bailiffs, interpreters, and service providers.

Overall

Judge Bower received positive reviews from the observer and all respondents. He presented as highly professional, notably in his ability to communicate well with all parties in court and in his courteous demeanor. The judge also demonstrated thoroughness and neutrality and held others accountable.

What most people said

The observer reported that the judge appeared very comfortable talking with people, interacting with them, and explaining information to them. The judge explained various charges to litigants, how they could make their payments, and the meaning of suspended jail time. He also took time to ensure they understood the information. In one case, when alerted that a defendant was hard of hearing, the judge immediately adjusted his voice and ensured the defendant could process his words. In another, he took the time to ensure the newer prosecutor understood the historical context of a particular matter. On occasion, he seemed to explain things a bit fast, but, in all, he conveyed information in an effective, energetic manner. Judge Bower displayed courteous interactions with all. He enthusiastically greeted a court interpreter in one case and in another ensured that he pronounced a defense attorney’s name correctly. He demonstrated a helpful and patient demeanor, in one matter, providing fine payment flexibility in response to a litigant’s health issues. The judge seemed comfortable and energetic in interacting with a multitude of parties.

The observer recorded that the judge demonstrated neutrality and had a careful manner. Before proceeding in one case, he made a point to ensure that a litigant had a chance to talk with the prosecutor. In another case, after presenting various options, the judge chose to give the litigant time to think about how they wanted to move forward. The judge also exhibited consistency and appeared interested in promoting neutrality. In a couple of matters, upon presenting and discussing various options for the litigant, he emphasized that he did not want to influence their decisions either way. He was also willing to hold people accountable. For example, he explained to a litigant that they were responsible for telling the court why they couldn’t meet a deadline. He also emphasized to an attorney the importance of holding litigants accountable to ensure compliance with the law.

Six of the twelve respondents emphasized the judge’s neutrality. Two described the judge as “very thorough.” Another said that he “upheld the law” and was “unbiased,” while another found that he “treats everybody exactly the same” and “doesn’t show favoritism.” One noticed that he was “good about understanding when things happen to people,” but, at a certain point, “he will hold them accountable” for their actions.

Six also drew attention to the judge’s communication skills. Two said that the judge was “clear,” with one of them indicating that he was also “concise.” Two others found that he explained information “well,” while another indicated that the judge was “very repetitive.” Another stated that he “explains in detail” all options.

Four respondents highlighted the judge’s respectful demeanor. Two described the judge as “understanding,” and two described the judge as “nice.” One shared that he was “really reasonable,” and “he doesn’t talk down to you. He has respect for people.” Another said that he “goes out of his way to be polite” and was “very patient.”

Three discussed the judge’s willingness to listen and hear people out. One stated that he was “willing to hear what people have to say,” while another added that the judge “made me feel like I could talk.”

Comments that stood apart

One respondent, who expressed positive sentiments about the judge, did indicate that the judge “was harsh” with a litigant who paid her fines late.

Biography (from JPEC)

Judge Brian E. Brower was appointed to the Clearfield City Justice Court in August 2015 and to the Sunset City and Morgan County Justice Courts in December 2016. Judge Brower earned a Bachelor's degree in English Literature with a minor in Criminal Justice from Weber State University. He then graduated from the S.J. Quinney College of Law at the University of Utah. Upon graduation and passing the bar, Judge Brower was hired as a Deputy County Attorney for the Weber County Attorney's Office. He later served as both the Murray City Prosecutor as well as the Layton City Prosecutor before being appointed as Clearfield's City Attorney in 2007. He served as City Attorney for eight years prior to appointment as the City's Justice Court Judge.
