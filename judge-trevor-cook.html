Hive judge-rubric grades

How to read the grades: A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the judge went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means the judge passed JPEC’s minimum but JPEC rates that area below the judge’s peers; F means below JPEC’s legal minimum score of 3.6. Every grade is taken from JPEC’s own published wording, the same way for all 57 judges. No grade is based on how any case came out, or on who appointed the judge.

JPEC measure: Procedural Fairness. Passed every minimum standard JPEC applies to this kind of evaluation (4 of 4), including procedural fairness.
2. Follows the lawN/A
JPEC measure: Legal Ability. JPEC does not survey part-time judges; not counted.
3. Temperament & integrityN/A
JPEC measure: Integrity & Judicial Temperament. Not surveyed; not counted.
4. Runs the court wellN/A
JPEC measure: Administrative Skills. Not surveyed; not counted.
5. Public recordN/A
H.B. 540 (2026) requires judges to file annual financial disclosures (board seats, investments, real estate, a spouse’s job). The Judicial Council is writing the rules and no judge has filed one yet, so this is not graded for anyone. It will be graded with the same missing-item ladder used for candidates’ disclosure forms.
6. Retention moneyN/A
A judge may form one retention campaign committee and must report each contribution to the Lieutenant Governor within 31 days; an interim report is due seven days before the election (Oct. 27, 2026). No committee for this judge was found as of Oct. 17, 2026. That is not a finding that none exists; it will be checked again after Oct. 27.
OverallC
Average of the graded questions (A=4 … F=0; N/A left out): 2.0. A plus or minus shows where the average falls between letters.

How a grade can change: Questions 1–4 change only with JPEC’s next evaluation of this judge. Questions 5 and 6 will be graded once judges’ H.B. 540 disclosures are posted or a retention committee files reports.

JPEC’s scores

Why there are no scores here: JPEC does not run surveys for part-time justice court judges because too few attorneys appear before them for a reliable sample. Instead, trained courtroom observers watch the judge. The judge must pass every minimum standard (4 of 4).

What JPEC’s report says

The text below is JPEC’s own public report, reproduced as published. Quotes inside it are from JPEC’s survey respondents and courtroom observers, not from the Hive.

JPEC summary

Together, the four observers were mixed about Judge Cook. Two observers were positive in their assessments. However, the other two were both mixed, each expressing positive sentiments but also sharing some concerns. Three of the four felt the judge could work on cultivating a more respectful demeanor (see “Widely Agreed Upon Themes,” “Other Observations,” and “Anomalous Comments”).  This judge meets discipline standards set by statute and has been certified by the Judicial Council as meeting all time standards, education requirements, and mental and physical competence standards.

Courtroom observers, overall

  • Together, the four observers were mixed about Judge Cook. Two observers were positive in their assessments, with one of them sharing additional comments. The other two were mixed, expressing positive sentiments but also sharing some concerns (see “Widely Agreed Upon Themes,” “Other Observations,” and “Anomalous Comments”).
  • Two observers reported that if they appeared before Judge Cook they would be treated fairly. The two others reported “mixed feelings,” with one reporting tentative confidence but the other concluding they “would not want to appear” before the judge.

What observers agreed on

  • Observers variously reported that Judge Cook demonstrated listening skills and attentiveness to defendants. He showed “proficient working knowledge of the law” and “was adept at allowing the communication to run smoothly between the defendants and the interpreter.” he appeared as business-like, patient, and flexible with court fine payments. He exhibited consideration of defendants’ various needs, in one case, recognizing that a person was “hard of hearing” and inviting them to come closer to the bench to talk with the judge. He worked diligently with prosecutors, was unhurried with interpreters, and ensured each defendant reviewed the rights form, “doing a quick review,” if necessary. He provided participants the chance to tell their stories and discussed issues with courtroom staff, “accepting their input.” The judge “explained charges, possible penalties, and addressed any confusion” and ensured that defendants understood their rights.
  • Three of the four observers suggested that the judge could work on fostering a more respectful demeanor. They variously described he manner as flat, somber, and abrupt. One noted that he “facial expression and body language could be more…welcoming…” Another suggested that instead of asking defendants “why they did what they did,” he should embrace a warmer approach by asking, “tell me your side, or share your side.”

Other observations

  • One commented positively about the court’s audio and video, but two gave negative feedback.
    • One stated that the “cameras were well positioned,” though “occasionally blurry,” and “the sound was fairly good.”
    • Two others had different viewing experiences, and both recorded several problems with the audio and video, particularly consistent audio clarity.
  • Two were favorable regarding the judge’s neutrality, but one expressed critical comments.
    • One observed “no obvious partiality exhibited” and found that he “[stuck] to guidelines for fines or other punishment which he applied equitably.” The second described him as neutral and found that he “[covered all the rules and procedures].”
    • The third observed that the judge was neutral and consistent “when an interpreter was involved,” but, overall, felt that he could work on being more consistent and neutral, particularly regarding the fine payments.

Comments that stood apart

  • One observer shared additional concerns about the judge:
    • While they noted that he tended to listen to responses to his own direct questions, the observer recounted an instance when a defendant was explaining what happened in their case, and the judge “didn't listen or even try to hear what the defendant was saying.”
    • They felt that he was “rushed with some individuals” and “tended to talk fast,” except when working with an interpreter.

Biography (from JPEC)

Appointed to the justice court bench in 2012, Judge Trevor L. Cook serves in the Nibley City, Mendon City and Rich County justice courts. Judge Cook graduated from Utah State University with a B.S. in management and human resources, and earned a law degree from Texas Southern University. He is also employed as in-house counsel.
