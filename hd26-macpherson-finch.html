Seven Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSOne of the closest House seats in Utah. The Republican incumbent is funded mostly by his caucus’s two PACs; the Democratic challenger by individuals, unions and advocacy groups. Both are checked the same way. Only MacPherson has a voting record.

  1. MacPherson raised $103,348.65 in 2026. $75,000 of it came from the Utah House Republicans Election Committee PAC ($40,000) and House Speaker Mike Schultz’s leadership PAC ($35,000). Individuals gave $2,447.65 in 26 gifts.
  2. Since his 2023 appointment, those two PACs have given him $132,500 ($80,000 and $52,500). He also lent his campaign $24,000 (2024–2025), and it paid him $10,000 back in March 2026.
  3. His largest vendor is Election Hive, a Republican campaign firm: $32,652.44 since 2024. In the Field LLC received $25,868.50 in 2024.
  4. His bills put new limits on courts. H.B. 392, signed, lets only the attorney general, the governor or the Legislature move a challenge to a state law to a three-judge panel. H.B. 600, which died, would have let voters overturn Utah Supreme Court rulings that strike down laws. His H.R. 8 censured a district judge (67–3).
  5. His disclosure form lists a staffing company, a small-business financing company and a fireworks importer and seller he’s a partner in; state records show the financing company dissolved in 2025, the staffing company inactive and the fireworks company active. TNT Fireworks gave his campaign $750 in 2024. He also listed Tesla and Traeger stock, income from Utah Power Credit Union, and, voluntarily, his lawyers.
  6. Finch raised $48,230.00 in 2026, 49.7% from 165 individual gifts (138 of them $100 or less). The House Democratic caucus PAC gave $6,500; Utah Women and Politics and UIN PAC $5,000 each; six unions $4,800.
  7. Finch’s form lists his West Valley City board seat and, as last year’s job, co-owner and operator of a PostNet store, but the item for companies he owned or ran in the past year says “NA.” He voluntarily listed his spouse’s former state employer.

MACPHERSON’S RECORD

Twenty Bills in 2026, Six Signed

IN PLAIN WORDSMacPherson was primary sponsor of 20 bills and five resolutions in 2026. Six bills were signed and two House resolutions passed; most of the rest passed the House and died in the Senate on the session’s last night. Finch has not held office.

H.B. 392 and the courts

H.B. 392 (2026), H.B. 600, H.J.R. 21 and H.R. 8

H.B. 392, “District Court Amendments.” Introduced as a new “Constitutional Court,” it was rewritten by substitute into three-judge district court panels. Per the compare document, the attorney general, the governor or the Legislature may file a “notice of statewide concern” to convene a panel, and that determination “may not be challenged by a party” and “is not subject to judicial review.” Normal venue rules don’t apply. The final substitute gives the Utah Supreme Court appeals from a panel. The House passed the final version 55–16 and the governor signed it Feb. 13, 2026. Sen. Mike McKell was Senate sponsor.

H.B. 600 would have let voters reject a Utah Supreme Court opinion finding a state law unconstitutional: signatures from 8% of active voters statewide and in 15 Senate districts, filed within days of the ruling, with the opinion stayed until an election. It died without a vote. H.J.R. 21, the matching court rules for panels, also died.

H.R. 8 censures Seventh District Judge Don M. Torgerson over his remarks, including “I’ve seen worse,” at a May 20, 2025 sentencing in a child sexual exploitation case. It passed the House 67–3 on March 3, 2026 and directs that copies go to the judge, the Judicial Conduct Commission and the Judicial Council. It needs no Senate vote. Sen. McKell called it “misguided,” per Utah News Dispatch.

The Utah State Bar warned the panel structure “invites strategic filings designed to obtain more favorable outcomes rather than impartial adjudication,” per Utah News Dispatch. The courts’ administrator said it would “apply more judicial minds to some of the toughest questions.” See also this outlet’s judiciary committee page.

His amendments to other members’ bills

S.B. 188 (2025) and H.B. 273 (2026)

S.B. 188, Sen. Keith Grover’s “School District Modifications,” was changed in the House Education Committee on Feb. 27, 2025 by an amendment MacPherson moved (12–0). It says a lease revenue bond issued by a new or reorganized district in these circumstances “is not subject to the combined total limitation.” Alpine’s $201 million lease revenue bonds followed. See Case 29 and the water and land tracker (grade F).

H.B. 273, Rep. Ariel Defay’s “Classroom Technology Amendments” (signed), carries a House amendment whose document lists MacPherson as its proposer. This outlet’s tracker grades the bill D, finding its instructional-technology standard was changed in a way that lets general-purpose tools qualify.

S.B. 188 from House Education Committee minutes (read for Case 29); H.B. 273 amendment document read through a web text reader
His other signed bills
  • H.B. 136, “Unlicensed Driver Amendments”: officers “shall seize and take possession of a vehicle” when the driver lacks credentials, with exceptions; quick fingerprints when identity can’t be verified; impound fee $425 to $600 and testing fee $30 to $60, with waivers. On the House floor he cited West Valley City data that 34% of crashes involved unlicensed drivers, per KSL. Final votes: House 55–12, Senate 20–7, House concurrence 57–13.
  • H.B. 539, “Public Safety Modifications”: domestic violence bail presumption, a wider definition of cohabitant, written release notices to victims. House 66–0; Senate 19–6; concurrence 53–10.
  • H.B. 148, “Tax Return Donation Amendments”: a School Meals Debt Relief Fund funded by tax-return donations. Sen. Luz Escamilla (D) was Senate sponsor.
  • H.B. 82, “Judgment Information Amendments”: judgment lien filings use the last four digits of a Social Security number and month and year of birth.
  • H.B. 314, “Firearm Purchase Amendments”: House 64–4, Senate 18–6. Its text was not read for this page and it is not graded.

Graded from the texts and compare documents read (see Limits). H.B. 314, S.B. 62, S.B. 304 and S.B. 64 are not graded here because their texts were not read or could not be separated into old and new language.

BillPowerTrans­parencyMoneyEnviron­mentCommun­ityOverall
S.B. 188 (2025) (his amendment)DDFN/ADF
Why: Grover’s bill. In the House Education Committee on Feb. 27, 2025, he moved the amendment exempting lease revenue bonds of a new or reorganized school district from the combined borrowing cap (12–0). The water tracker grades the bill F.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Leaving lease revenue bonds under the cap, or requiring a recorded roll call on the change.
H.B. 392 (2026)DCN/AN/ADD
Why: Signed Feb. 13, 2026. Creates three-judge district court panels for cases challenging state laws. Only the attorney general, the governor or the Legislature can file the notice that convenes one, and per the compare document that choice “is not subject to judicial review.” Panel judges are drawn from different districts, and the Utah Supreme Court hears appeals.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Letting any party request a panel on the same terms, and allowing review of the decision to convene one.
H.B. 600 (2026)DCN/AN/ADD
Why: Would have let voters overturn a Utah Supreme Court ruling that a state law is unconstitutional; the ruling would not take effect while signatures were gathered and until voters approved it. Died without a vote.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Keeping a court ruling in effect while any referendum proceeds.
H.J.R. 21 (2026)DCN/AN/ADD
Why: Companion court rules for H.B. 392’s panels: state officials may file notice within 45 days to move a case to a panel, and parties may not disqualify a whole panel. Died without a vote.
What would have scored higher for the collective: The same as H.B. 392: equal access for all parties.
H.R. 4 (2026)CDN/AN/ACD
Why: As introduced, a sponsor “shall verbally disclose” in committee and on the floor when a bill came from an agency head’s request. The version that passed the House says a representative “may disclose” it when filing the bill request, and staff count how often they do.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Keeping the required disclosure in committee and on the floor.
H.B. 302 (2026)DCCN/ADD
Why: Would have limited local governments to one official flag, required a legislative committee to review any other, and let the state auditor fine violators $500 a day. Passed the House 57–12; died in the Senate.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Leaving flag choices to local councils, with public notice.
H.B. 136 (2026)CCDN/ACC
Why: Signed. Requires officers to seize vehicles of drivers without valid credentials, requires quick fingerprints when identity can’t be verified, and raises the impound fee from $425 to $600 and a testing fee from $30 to $60, with waivers for stolen vehicles. He cited unlicensed-driver crash data from West Valley City on the House floor.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Publishing yearly counts of seizures, fingerprints and waivers by city.
H.B. 593 (2026)DCBN/ACC
Why: Would have created a pretrial release task force, required detention orders for certain felony arrestees, and sent part of forfeited bail to the indigent defense account. Passed the House 56–15; died in the Senate.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Keeping a judge’s case-by-case discretion alongside the new criteria.
H.B. 407 (2026)DBCN/ACC
Why: Would have required one state-procured student information system by 2031 unless a district kept its own, with Legislative Auditor audits and a 10% pay cut for district leaders who didn’t comply. Passed the House 72–1; died in the Senate.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Letting districts show compliance without the pay penalty.
H.B. 581 (2026)CCCCCC
Why: Would have made manufacturers responsible for traffic-law compliance when a self-driving system is engaged, and set up an autonomous transit pilot before the 2034 Winter Games. Passed the House 61–9; died in the Senate.
What would have scored higher for the collective: Public reporting of pilot-program crashes and costs.
H.B. 601 (2026)CCN/AN/ACC
Why: Would have narrowed which flavored malt drinks grocery and convenience stores may sell as beer. Introduced and filed the same day, March 6.
What would have scored higher for the collective: A committee hearing before the session’s last day.
H.B. 455 (2026)CCN/AN/ABC
Why: Would have let police and dispatcher applicants whose non-violent, non-sexual convictions were expunged or dismissed at least 10 years earlier attend the academy. Died.
H.B. 487 (2026)CBN/AN/ACC
Why: Would have stated that legislators may introduce and vote on bills by their own judgment, and limited legislative staff’s correction power to technical errors that don’t change a law’s meaning. Passed the House 64–0; died in the Senate.
H.R. 8 (2026)CBN/AN/ACC
Why: Censures Seventh District Judge Don M. Torgerson over remarks at a May 2025 sentencing. Passed the House 67–3 on a recorded vote and directs that a copy go to the Judicial Conduct Commission, the body that reviews complaints about judges. It changes no law.
H.B. 539 (2026)CBN/AN/ABB
Why: Signed. Domestic violence changes: a presumption against bail for a new domestic violence offense while on probation or parole for one, and written notice of release conditions to victims.
H.B. 82 (2026)CBN/AN/ABB
Why: Signed. Judgment liens list only the last four digits of a debtor’s Social Security number and the month and year of birth.
H.B. 148 (2026)CBBN/ABB
Why: Signed. Creates a School Meals Debt Relief Fund that taxpayers can give to on their tax returns, with district reporting and proportional payouts.
H.B. 604 (2026)BCN/AN/ACB
Why: Would have barred state executive officials and county officials from sitting on a city council, and city officials from a county council. Passed the House 43–23; died in the Senate.
