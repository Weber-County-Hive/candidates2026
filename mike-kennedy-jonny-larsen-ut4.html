The Race

Rep. Mike Kennedy (R), currently serving Utah's 3rd Congressional District, is running for re-election in the newly drawn 4th Congressional District for the November 3, 2026 general election. His opponent is Jonny Larsen (D), a Marine Corps veteran making his first run for federal office.

Why Kennedy is running in a different district: Utah's congressional map was thrown out by a Utah Third District Court judge in late 2025 after finding the Legislature's 2021 map ignored the state's voter-approved redistricting standards. A federal panel later declined to block the court-ordered replacement map from taking effect for 2026. Under the new lines, three of Utah's four sitting House Republicans are running in different-numbered districts than the ones they currently hold — Kennedy is one of them, moving from CD3 into the new CD4. Rep. Burgess Owens, who previously held CD4, did not make this year's ballot.
Note on scope: Kennedy is an incumbent with a longer prior public record — a full term in Congress plus multiple terms in the Utah House and Senate before that, along with legislative votes, prior campaign cycles, and committee assignments — that naturally generates more to document than a first-time candidate has yet created. Coverage volume here reflects the record each candidate has actually produced, not a decision to scrutinize one campaign more than the other.

The Candidates

Rep. Mike Kennedy (R)

First-term incumbent, U.S. House (currently UT-3, seeking re-election in the new UT-4; sworn in Jan. 2025). Physician and attorney. Previously served three terms in the Utah House, District 27 (2013–2019), then two terms in the Utah Senate (2021–2025, redistricted from the 14th to the 21st district partway through). Ran unsuccessfully for U.S. Senate in 2018 (lost to Mitt Romney). Sponsors a leadership PAC, Heal America.

Jonny Larsen (D)

Fifth-generation Utahn, grew up in Salt Lake City's Fairpark neighborhood on food stamps and free school lunch; entered foster care with his younger brother at 13. Raised largely by his grandmother, a Teamsters Local 222 member whose union pension, Medicare, and Social Security kept the household stable. Served as a Marine Corps Infantry Rifleman (2000–2004, Sergeant), including two Iraq deployments — the initial push into Baghdad and the Battle of Husaybah — and was stationed in Washington, D.C. during the 9/11 Pentagon attack. Worked in IT as a hospital systems/network administrator after his service, then used the GI Bill to study Applied Math and Physics at the University of Utah before the funding ran out. First-time federal candidate; won the Democratic convention field for UT-4. (Biographical detail per his own campaign website — not independently verified beyond what's stated there.)

Burt's Platform

The following is drawn from Burt's own campaign website — his stated positions, not independently fact-checked. He is running as an unaffiliated/independent candidate.

Why independent

Burt held his campaign kickoff event, titled "Our America," on July 2, 2026 — deliberately timed to the Second Continental Congress's actual independence vote (distinct from July 4's Declaration signing), tying into his 250th-anniversary "Declare Your Independence" branding.

Burt frames his core beliefs around the Constitution, unalienable rights, federalism, limited government, separation of powers, and rule of law — and states he is unaffiliated because he views both major parties as more committed to their own power than to those principles. He also states a belief that representatives should live in the districts they represent and stay closely familiar with constituents' needs — consistent with his own South Jordan residency claim above.

A separate page on his site ("Declare Your Independence") elaborates this at length, styled after the Declaration of Independence and citing historical warnings against partisanship from Washington's Farewell Address, plus Adams, Jefferson, and Hamilton. His specific list of grievances includes: inflation and housing affordability, an unreformed immigration system, unaddressed healthcare costs, unregulated AI risk, social media harms to young people, self-enrichment by elected officials, the absence of term limits, a call for accountability in the Jeffrey Epstein case, and Congress's chronic reliance on continuing resolutions rather than passing spending bills on time (he cites this pattern as unbroken since 1997). He commits to five specific actions: registering unaffiliated, ceasing support for either major party, committing to nonviolent political discourse, opposing rules that disadvantage independent/third-party candidates, and recommitting to founding constitutional principles.

The Epstein-accountability line is a notably unusual plank for a congressional campaign platform — worth flagging as distinctive rather than a standard talking point.

Direct exchange with Kennedy — wildfire policy

Kennedy published a Deseret News opinion piece (Aug. 2, 2026, "Empowering local officials to fight and prevent wildfires their way") citing 743 wildfires and 387,000+ acres burned in Utah so far in the 2026 season, noting federal agencies oversee roughly 70% of Utah's land, and proposing his own bills (Cross-Boundary Wildfire Solutions Act, Accelerating Forest Management Act, Forest Health and Wildfire Risk Reduction Act, and his TORCH Act — Targeted Operations to Remove Catastrophic Hazards Act, named for the late Rep. Doug LaMalfa) plus expanded Good Neighbor Authority as the fix. Kennedy wrote: "The federal government can help, but will never understand or care for this land the way Utahns do" — confirmed verbatim against his original piece, not a mischaracterization.

Burt responded with his own opinion piece, agreeing with Kennedy's Good Neighbor Authority point but arguing the diagnosis is incomplete on two fronts: (1) citing the Center for Western Priorities' claim that the Forest Service treated roughly 35% fewer acres for hazardous fuels in 2025 than 2024, with some prescribed burns cancelled after crews had already traveled to perform them, and a cited ~43% Forest Service funding cut referenced at a June 25, 2026 House Agriculture Committee hearing; and (2) Kennedy's piece not mentioning climate change, where Burt cites a NOAA finding that "climate change, including increased heat, extended drought, and a thirsty atmosphere, has been a key driver" of increased western wildfire risk. Burt also describes personally learning about "wildfire spotting" (embers traveling up to a mile) from an incident-management liaison officer during the Iron Fire near Eureka.

Kennedy's quote and cited statistics are confirmed against his own published piece. Burt's cited figures (Center for Western Priorities, the June 2026 hearing, Taxpayers for Common Sense, NOAA) are attributed to those sources in his own piece but have not yet been independently re-verified against the primary sources themselves.

Stated priorities

In order as listed on his own site: restoring affordability/the "American Dream" for average earners as his top priority; immigration system reform paired with a specific critique that "ICE is out of control," calling instead for ICE to focus on dangerous criminals rather than broader enforcement; a critique of what he calls "intellectuals" within the MAGA movement who he says are critical of America's founding principles, calling for recommitment to those principles instead; and ending the two-party system outright, which he frames under a "Declare Your Independence" banner.

Worth noting for balance: his ICE-focused immigration position and his anti-two-party framing both cut against standard partisan lines in either direction — neither a conventional Republican nor Democratic position as stated.

State-Federal Alignment & Additional Findings

Six findings connecting Kennedy's federal record to Utah's own state legislative history, each graded against this file's standing five-part rubric (Power, Transparency, Financial Accountability, Environmental Impact, Community Impact).

These grades assess the collective impact of each bill or vote itself — not a character judgment of the candidate who sponsored or voted for it. A specific transaction, vote, or deal is graded on its own terms, independent of that person's other conduct.

1. H.B. 502 (2024) — Kennedy's own prior vote on mining/zoningB

As introduced in the Utah Legislature, H.B. 502 ("Critical Infrastructure and Mining") mandated that by Dec. 31, 2024, every political subdivision's zoning ordinances allow mining/critical-infrastructure operations as a permitted use — overriding existing overlay zoning, barring subdivisions from "unreasonably limiting" these operations, and deeming any non-compliant subdivision to allow it anyway. The House Natural Resources Committee replaced this entirely with a 1st Substitute the same day (2/22/24) after testimony from Millcreek Mayor Jeff Silvestrini, the Utah League of Cities and Towns, and the League of Women Voters — all pushing for the study-only replacement. What passed instead was just a $500,000 state-agency study, with no binding mandate on local governments.

The bill remained contested at every stage even after the mandate was stripped: House 3rd reading 57-16-2, Senate committee 5-2-1, Senate floor 19-9-1, House concurrence 54-17-4 (crossing party lines both times). Then-Sen. Mike Kennedy voted yes at the Senate floor stage — the same Kennedy now in Congress sponsoring H.R. 1501, a federal mining-permitting bill. This is a documented, two-year-earlier data point showing his interest in mining/critical-infrastructure permitting policy predates his current committee assignment.

Why it changed, stated plainly: the zoning-override mandate did not survive because a sitting mayor, the statewide municipal association, and citizen/League of Women Voters testimony pushed back on it in open committee — and that pushback succeeded before any floor vote happened. Kennedy's yes vote came at the Senate floor stage, after the override was already gone. He voted for the compromise that citizen and local-government pressure produced, not for the override itself. This is a case where the public process worked, and he backed the outcome of it — not a vote against local control.

PowerFails as introduced Holds as enacted The introduced bill would have stripped local zoning authority outright; organized local-government pushback got it replaced before passage.
TransparencyPasses The substitution happened in open committee testimony with named witnesses on the record, not a hidden late-session swap.
Financial AccountabilityPasses Straightforward $500,000 line-item appropriation for a study; fiscal note confirms no local government cost.
Environmental ImpactPasses The enacted version imposes no zoning override, so no environmental-review bypass occurred.
Community ImpactPasses A sitting mayor and the statewide municipal association successfully represented local government interests against a state mandate.

Overall: Grade B — a bad design caught and fixed by real public process, not a quiet reversal.

2. S.B. 254 (2026) ↔ H.R. 1501 — state and federal mining permitting moving togetherDC

Utah's S.B. 254 sets a goal of cutting critical-minerals permitting timelines to under 18 months and creates "critical minerals zones" with fast-tracked parallel state permitting. Kennedy's federal H.R. 1501 does the same thing at the federal level — codifying mining as a FAST-41 "covered project" eligible for expedited environmental review. Both moved through their respective legislatures in the same 2026 window. This isn't just parallel policy on paper: the Interior Department already approved Utah's Velvet-Wood uranium-vanadium mine (San Juan County) under a new accelerated 14-day federal environmental review, tied to a national energy emergency declaration.

PowerFails S.B. 254 bars local governments from offering incentives outside state-designated zones and seats the mining industry association's president on the governing council; H.R. 1501 extends a federal fee/permitting framework industry itself asked to keep in place.
TransparencyFails (S.B. 254) Passes (H.R. 1501) S.B. 254 was renamed and substantively rewritten in a 9-hour window the same day it had already passed both Utah legislative chambers under a different name. H.R. 1501 went through an ordinary committee/floor process.
Financial AccountabilityMixed S.B. 254's mining-exploration tax credit caps were reduced in substitute, but its severance-tax transfer was redirected to a broader account than originally proposed. H.R. 1501 is industry-fee-funded, not taxpayer-funded.
Environmental ImpactFails Both bills exist specifically to shorten environmental review timelines for mining — that is their stated purpose, not a side effect.
Community ImpactMixed Real economic development goals (critical minerals independence, jobs) exist alongside real speed-vs-scrutiny tradeoffs for communities near new mining activity.

Overall: S.B. 254 Grade D (per this publication's standing bill tracker); H.R. 1501 not separately graded elsewhere, assessed here as a Grade C — narrower in scope than S.B. 254 and procedurally clean, but sharing the same environmental-review-speed tradeoff.

3. Outdoor Americans with Disabilities Act ↔ Utah's OHV legislative buildoutC

Kennedy's bill (House companion to Sen. Mike Lee's S. 2968) would require federal land managers to keep at least 2.5 miles of motorized routes open per square mile of "disability-accessible" land, restricting road closures. The groups that pushed it in the House were ORBA and SEMA — off-road business associations, not disability-advocacy organizations. Utah has spent eight years building the funding and legal infrastructure this bill would benefit: HB143 (2018) standardized OHV registration/fees; HB180 (2022) gave the OHV community enforcement tools; HB409 (2022) dedicated ~$36 million in ongoing sales-tax revenue to recreation infrastructure; HB125 (2022) made the Outdoor Recreational Infrastructure Grant Program permanent.

PowerFails Removes federal land managers' discretion to close or restrict motorized routes, the one lever Utah's own funding buildout can't reach on its own.
TransparencyPasses Ordinary bill introduction and companion-legislation process; no hidden retitling or scope change found.
Financial AccountabilityN/A No direct federal spending attached; Utah's own funding (HB409/HB125) is separately appropriated and already transparent.
Environmental ImpactMixed Mandating minimum motorized-route density can conflict with wildlife/habitat management goals on the same land; no environmental review of the tradeoff is built into the bill.
Community ImpactMixed Genuine accessibility framing exists, but the bill's actual advocacy base is the off-road recreation industry, not disability-rights organizations — a real gap between stated purpose and who is actually driving it.

Overall: Grade C — a real accessibility rationale attached to what is functionally industry-requested access expansion.

PowerMixed Both laws condition public funding/contracts on an institution's political/commercial conduct — a real use of government funding leverage over speech-adjacent decisions, regardless of which side one takes on the underlying issue.
TransparencyPasses Both bills went through open, named-sponsor legislative processes with no hidden retitling.
Financial AccountabilityN/A Neither bill involves a direct spending or appropriation question.
Environmental ImpactN/A Not applicable to this subject matter.

5. Two bait-and-switch votes: the whale bill and the AGOA shell swapF

H.R. 9436 was introduced neutrally as extending "certain regulations concerning the North Atlantic right whale." What it actually does, per the committee's own report: delays new whale-entanglement protection rules from 2028 to 2035 — a 13-year waiver from Endangered Species Act and Marine Mammal Protection Act requirements — while reducing incentives for lobstermen to adopt safer gear. It was renamed mid-process to the "Northeast Lobsterman Protection Act of 2026," reframing a delay of protections as protecting workers. Kennedy voted yes.

H.R. 6500 started as the bipartisan "AGOA Extension Act" (African trade preferences), passing the House 340-54 in January 2026. The Senate later struck the entire bill and replaced it with a full government-funding continuing resolution — a completely different subject. Kennedy's Sept. 1, 2026 vote, still labeled "AGOA Extension Act" on his own House website, was actually a vote to fund the government using AGOA's bill number as the vehicle.

PowerFails (both) Each bypassed the normal expectation that a bill's substance matches what members and the public are told they're voting on.
TransparencyFails (both) The whale bill's rename obscures what it delays; the AGOA vote is still mislabeled on official vote records months after its content changed entirely.
Financial AccountabilityN/A Neither case is primarily a spending-disclosure issue (the appropriations content swapped into H.R. 6500 would need its own separate review).
Environmental ImpactFails (whale bill only) A direct, quantified delay of species-protection regulation with an acknowledged conservation cost.
Community ImpactMixed (whale bill) N/A (AGOA) Real economic benefit to Maine's lobster industry, at a documented ecological cost to right whale recovery.

Overall: Grade F on both — not for their underlying subject matter, but for the mismatch between what each bill is called and what it does.

6. The broader pattern: delaying or bypassing environmental reviewPATTERN

H.R. 1501 (mining permitting), the Utah/Nevada land-sale amendment, and H.R. 9436 (whale protection delay) all share the same underlying move: delay or bypass an existing environmental review requirement to speed up industry activity. Utah's own H.B. 502 (as introduced, before it was stripped) and S.B. 254 do the identical thing at the state level — override or fast-track past standard review processes for mining specifically. This is a consistent theme across Kennedy's votes and Utah's own recent legislative direction, not a coincidence limited to one bill.

PowerFails Recurring removal or delay of existing procedural checks, across multiple bills and both levels of government.
TransparencyMixed Varies by instance — some (H.R. 1501, H.B. 502 as enacted) went through ordinary process; others (the land-sale amendment, the whale bill's rename, S.B. 254's same-day rewrite) did not.
Financial AccountabilityN/A Not the primary axis of this pattern.
Environmental ImpactFails Every instance in this pattern trades review rigor for speed, by design.
Community ImpactMixed Real economic development arguments exist in every case; real environmental/community costs are also documented in every case. Neither side of this tradeoff is invented.

This is a pattern-level observation across multiple already-graded bills, not a new bill with its own overall letter grade.

What this pattern is, and isn't, evidence of: what's documented here is parallel timing and shared subject matter — state and federal mining/land-use permitting policy moving in the same direction, in the same general window. That is not evidence of coordination or intent between Kennedy and specific Utah officials or industry figures; no communication, meeting, or agreement between them has been found. Questions worth asking before treating this as more than a pattern: Did Kennedy or his office have any direct contact with the sponsors of S.B. 254 or H.B. 502? Did any shared industry group (e.g., the Critical Infrastructure Materials Coalition, named in the H.B. 502 committee record) lobby both the state legislature and Kennedy's federal office on these bills? Is the timing coincidental, driven by a shared national trend (e.g., the administration's energy-emergency declaration), or actually coordinated? None of this is answered by what's in this file — it's a pattern to keep watching, not a conclusion to publish.

Estimated net worth from these ranges is roughly $1.4 million (per NOTUS's and Quiver Quantitative's independent range-based calculations from the same disclosure) — placing him well below the median for a sitting member of Congress. In plain terms: House financial disclosures only require reporting in ranges, not exact figures, so outside groups estimate net worth by working through those ranges themselves — and both groups independently landed on roughly the same number. Congress as a body skews wealthy, with many members worth several million dollars or more, so $1.4 million puts Kennedy on the lower end of his colleagues, not the average or above it — worth noting plainly, since it cuts against any assumption that his committee work or stock purchases are about personal enrichment on an unusual scale.

House financial disclosures report values in ranges rather than exact figures, so all amounts above are ranges as filed, not precise dollar values.

Congressional candidates must file a financial disclosure report with the Clerk of the House within 30 days of becoming a candidate, generally no later than May 15, with a possible extension to August 15. Public advocacy on OBBBA's solar provisions

On LinkedIn, Burt publicly urged people to call their U.S. Senators while OBBBA was still pending, arguing the bill should be improved to support "all forms of energy, including solar" rather than an "All of the Above" strategy that excludes it. This is a direct, on-the-record policy position taken before the bill's final passage — and it puts him at odds with the version Kennedy ultimately voted for, which phased out the IRA-era solar tax credits already documented in this case file's OBBBA/environmental-impact section.
